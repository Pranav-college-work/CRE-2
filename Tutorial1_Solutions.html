(ii) Synthesis techniques

  • Precipitation and co-precipitation — metal-salt solutions mixed with a base at controlled pH and temperature, then filtered, washed, dried, calcined. The standard route to Cu/ZnO/Al2O3 (methanol, LT shift) and Fe–Cr shift catalysts. Gives high loading and intimate mixing of components.
  • Impregnation — incipient-wetness (pore-volume) or wet impregnation of a preformed support, then drying, calcination and reduction or sulfidation. This is precisely the route in JM Smith Example 8-5 (Q6 below): alumina particles soaked in aqueous Ni(NO3)2, dried and reduced to ~7 wt% NiO. Also used for Co–Mo/Al2O3, Pt/Al2O3, Ag/α-Al2O3.
  • Ion exchange — the zeolite route: Na-Y → NH4-Y → H-Y; rare-earth exchange (REY) and steam dealumination to USY for FCC; Cu/Fe exchange for SCR catalysts.
  • Hydrothermal (sol–gel) crystallisation — a silica–alumina gel with a structure-directing template, autoclaved at 100–180 °C, then calcined to burn out the template. Zeolite Y, ZSM-5, SAPO-34.
  • Fusion / melting — magnetite with promoters melted at ~1600 °C, cooled, crushed and sieved. This is how the ammonia-synthesis catalyst is made, and it is why its shape is “irregular granules”.
  • Spray drying and flame/spray pyrolysis — spray drying produces the 20–150 µm FCC microspheres of Q2. Note that JM Smith Example 8-1 uses spray drying to argue that no non-porous particle route can deliver hundreds of m2/g — the argument you reproduce in Q2.
  • Shaping and forming — extrusion (lobed hydrotreating extrudates), tabletting/pelletting (the pelleting-pressure vs macroporosity trade-off of JMS Table 8-3, directly relevant to Q6), oil-drop spheroidisation, and washcoating of monoliths.
  • Deposition–precipitation, grafting, CVD/ALD — for very well-dispersed noble metals and atomically thin overlayers.

Q2

External surface area per unit volume of a 100 µm spherical Zeolite Y FCC catalyst; and the size needed for an external area of 320 m2/g. Density 1.5 g/cm3.

2 marks

The idea behind the question

This is JM Smith Example 8-1 restated for an FCC microsphere. Its whole purpose is to establish, by a one-line calculation, that the external (geometric) surface of a catalyst particle is negligible. The hundreds of m2/g that a working catalyst needs cannot come from the outside of particles — they must come from the internal pore walls. That single conclusion is what makes the rest of Chapter 8 (pore volume, BET area, pore-size distribution) necessary, and what makes intraparticle diffusion a first-order concern in CRE-II rather than a refinement.

Derivation

For a sphere of diameter dp, the external area per unit particle volume is

Sv= πdp2 πdp3/6 =6dp

and dividing by the particle density ρp converts it to an area per unit mass:

Sg= Svρp = 6ρpdp

valid for non-porous (or externally-counted) spheres only

Part (a) — area per unit volume of a 100 µm microsphere

dp = 100 µm = 100×10−4 cm = 1×10−2 cm = 1×10−4 m  ·  ρp = 1.5 g/cm3
Sv= 61×10−4 m =6×104  m2 /m3

Expressed per gram this is

Sg= 61.5×1×10−2 =400 cm2/g =0.04 m2/g

Read that number. A real FCC catalyst has Sg ≈ 150–300 m2/g. The external surface contributes 0.04 m2/g — about 0.02 % of the total. Better than 99.9 % of a cracking catalyst’s surface is inside its pores. This is the quantitative justification for the statement in JMS §8-6 that “the external surface of the particles… is negligible”.

Part (b) — particle size required for an external area of 320 m2/g

Required Sg = 320 m2/g = 320×104 cm2/g = 3.2×106 cm2/g

Invert Sg = 6/(ρpdp):

dp= 6ρpSg = 61.5×3.2×106 =1.25×10−6 cm

= 1.25 × 10−8 m = 12.5 nm = 0.0125 µm (radius 6.25×10−7 cm)

Answer

(a) Sv = 6/dp = 6×104 m2/m3 (= 600 cm2/cm3), equivalent to only 0.04 m2/g.

(b) To reach 320 m2/g from external area alone the particles would have to be dp = 1.25×10−6 cm = 12.5 nm (radius 6.25×10−7 cm).

Conclusion: 12.5 nm particles cannot be manufactured, handled or fluidised on a commercial scale — they would be blown straight out of the regenerator. Therefore the required area must be supplied by internal porosity, which is exactly what Zeolite Y provides through its 7.4 Å supercage windows and ~13 Å supercages.

Note on the printed answer key

The tutorial key quotes 3×104 m2/m3 and 6.25×10−7 cm. Both are exactly one-half of the diameter-based results above, i.e. the key consistently reads the stated 100 µm as a radius and reports the answer to part (b) as a radius as well. With R = 100 µm, dp = 200 µm and Sv = 6/(2×10−4) = 3×104 m2/m3; and 6.25×10−7 cm is the radius corresponding to the diameter 1.25×10−6 cm found above. Both conventions give the same physics — state which one you are using. Since the problem says “100 microns in diameter”, the diameter-based numbers (6×104 m2/m3, dp = 1.25×10−6 cm) are the literally correct ones, and the key’s value is recovered by quoting the radius.

Q3

Langmuir isotherm for CO2 on Zeolite-13X at 40 °C and 100 °C: evaluate qmax and K.

—

Book background — JM Smith §8-4, “The Langmuir Treatment of Adsorption”

Langmuir’s derivation rests on four assumptions, and every one of them matters when you judge the fit at the end:

  1. Energetically uniform surface — all sites adsorb with the same heat of adsorption. (Real 13X has a distribution of site energies; the strongest cation sites bind CO2 first.)
  2. No interaction between adsorbed molecules — the amount already adsorbed does not change the rate of adsorption per remaining site.
  3. One mechanism, one adsorbed complex — every adsorbed species has the same structure.
  4. Coverage does not exceed one monolayer, and each site holds one molecule.

Under these assumptions, the rate of adsorption is proportional to the pressure and to the bare fraction of the surface, and the rate of desorption to the covered fraction:

ra=kp(1−θ) , rd=k′θ

Equating them at equilibrium and writing K = k/k′ gives the Langmuir isotherm. Since the coverage θ is proportional to the amount adsorbed, θ = q/qmax:

θ=Kp1+Kp ⇒ q=qmaxKp1+Kp

Physical meaning of the two parameters. qmax is the saturation capacity — the loading at complete monolayer coverage, fixed by the number of adsorption sites per gram, so it is essentially a structural property of the zeolite and should be only weakly temperature-dependent. K is the adsorption equilibrium constant, units of (pressure)−1; it measures affinity, is the ratio of adsorption to desorption rate constants, and because adsorption is exothermic it must decrease with temperature. Note the two limits: at low p, Kp « 1 and q → qmaxKp (Henry’s law, linear); at high p, q → qmax (plateau).

Linearisation

JMS Eq. (8-8) rearranges the isotherm into a straight line. Dividing through by p and inverting:

pq= 1qmaxK + pqmax

plot p/q against p → slope s = 1/qmax, intercept i = 1/(qmaxK), hence qmax = 1/s and K = s/i

Use this Hanes form rather than the Lineweaver–Burk form (1/q vs 1/p). The latter puts enormous weight on the lowest-pressure point, where the measurement is least reliable; for this data set it gives a physically impossible negative qmax at 40 °C.

Checks that earn marks

  • K falls with temperature (6.27 → 3.17 atm−1), as it must: adsorption is exothermic, so raising T shifts the equilibrium back to the gas phase. This is visible in the raw data too — at every pressure, the 100 °C loading is lower.
  • qmax is nearly temperature-independent (4.33 → 3.72 mmol/g, a 14 % drop). It should be, since it counts sites. The residual decrease reflects the surface energetic heterogeneity of real 13X, which the Langmuir model does not carry.
  • Heat of adsorption from a two-point van’t Hoff fit of K:
    lnK2K1 =−ΔHadsR (1T2−1T1) ⇒ ΔHads≈−11 kJ/mol
    Negative, i.e. exothermic — the required sign. (The magnitude is low compared with the −35 to −45 kJ/mol usually reported for CO2 on 13X, because a two-point estimate from a lumped Langmuir K over a heterogeneous surface is crude. Comment on it; do not hide it.)
  • Where the model strains. The largest deviation is at 0.13 atm and 40 °C (2.40 measured vs 1.94 predicted). Real CO2–13X uptake rises more steeply than Langmuir at low coverage because the strongest Na+ sites fill first. A Toth or dual-site Langmuir isotherm, which admits site heterogeneity, fits this system markedly better — and that is what the source paper actually uses.
Q4

From the BET plot, estimate the surface area per gram of silica gel using (a) N2 at −195.8 °C and (b) O2 at −183 °C.

6 marks

Book background — JM Smith §8-5, “Determination of Surface Area”

Langmuir stops at one monolayer. Real physisorption isotherms keep climbing, because once the first layer is laid down, further molecules condense on top of it. Brunauer, Emmett and Teller extended Langmuir’s site balance to an infinite stack of layers, with the heat of adsorption in the first layer differing from the heat of liquefaction in all the rest. The result is the BET equation, JMS Eq. (8-9):

pv(p0−p) = 1vmC + (C−1)pCvmp0

where p0 is the saturation vapour pressure at the adsorption temperature, v is the volume adsorbed (at STP), vm the volume corresponding to one complete monolayer, and C a constant for that gas–solid pair. So a plot of p/[v(p0−p)] against p/p0 is a straight line — and, crucially, one that can safely be extrapolated back to p/p0 = 0. From JMS Eqs. (8-10) to (8-12):

i=1vmC , s=C−1vmC ⇒ vm=1i+s , C=1+si

Converting the monolayer volume to an area needs Avogadro’s number and the area α occupied by one adsorbed molecule — JMS Eq. (8-13):

Sg= vmN0Vα (N0=6.02×1023,V=22400 cm3/mol)

and α itself comes from close-packing the liquefied adsorbate — JMS Eq. (8-14):

α=1.09 [MN0ρ] 2/3

Check the two given α values with this. For N2 at −195.8 °C the liquid density is 0.808 g/cm3: α = 1.09[28/(6.02×1023×0.808)]2/3 = 16.2×10−16 cm2. For O2 at −183 °C, liquid density 1.14 g/cm3: α = 1.09[32/(6.02×1023×1.14)]2/3 = 14.2×10−16 cm2. Both match the values quoted in the question — worth showing, it is free marks.

Two convenient points on the line: (0.05, 2.0×10−3) and (0.30, 7.0×10−3)

and C = 1 + s/i = 21 — a healthy value; BET is reliable for C roughly between 20 and 200

Q5

JM Smith Example 8-4 — pore volume, solid density and particle porosity of activated silica from helium–mercury displacement.

3 marks

Book background — JM Smith §8-6, “Void Volume and Solid Density”

BET tells you how much surface there is. It says nothing about where it is or how open the structure is. For that you need the void volume, and the classical way to get it is the helium–mercury method:

  • Helium is small and non-adsorbing at room temperature, so it penetrates the pores. The volume of helium displaced by the sample is therefore the volume of the solid skeleton only.
  • Mercury does not wet oxides, and at atmospheric pressure it will not enter pores smaller than about 10 µm. So the volume of mercury displaced is the whole envelope volume of the particles — solid plus pores.
  • The difference is the pore volume. This is the entire trick.
Vg= VHg−VHem , ρs=mVHe , ρp=mVHg

and the porosity follows from JMS Eq. (8-16), or equivalently Eq. (8-17):

εp= void (pore) volume of particletotal volume of particle = VgρsVgρs+1 =Vgρp

Given

Activated silica, granular, 4 to 12 mesh
Mass of sample in the chamber, m = 101.5 g
Volume of helium displaced, VHe = 45.1 cm3
Volume of mercury displaced, VHg = 82.7 cm3

Solution

Pore volume per gram — mercury volume minus helium volume, per gram of sample:

Vg= 82.7−45.1101.5 =37.6101.5 =0.371 cm3/g

Density of the solid phase — the helium volume is the skeleton volume:

ρs=101.545.1=2.25 g/cm3

Density of the porous particles (not asked explicitly, but it is one division and it lets you cross-check the porosity):

ρp=101.582.7=1.227 g/cm3

Porosity, from Eq. (8-16):

εp= 0.371×2.250.371×2.25+1 =0.8341.834=0.455

cross-check with Eq. (8-17): εp = Vgρp = 0.371 × 1.227 = 0.455 ✓

Q6

JM Smith Example 8-5 — bidisperse Ni/Al2O3 hydrogenation pellet: densities, macro- and micropore void fractions, solid fraction, particle porosity.

4 marks

Book background — bidisperse pore structure

Fine catalyst particles are pelleted to 1/16–1/4 in. to keep the bed pressure drop down and give mechanical strength. Pelleting creates a two-level (bidisperse) pore structure:

  • Micropores — the original pores inside each alumina particle. In JMS’s convention (Table 8-3) these are radii below ~100 Å. They carry essentially all the surface area, hence all the reaction.
  • Macropores — the voids between the particles, created by the pelleting operation, radii above ~100 Å. They carry almost no area but they are the highways: reactants travel down macropores and then diffuse sideways into micropores.

Pelleting pressure trades one against the other. JMS Table 8-3 shows that as the pelleting pressure rises, the macropore volume collapses from 0.670 to 0.120 cm3/g while the micropore volume barely moves (0.434 to 0.365) — you crush the space between particles, not the pores within them. This is the whole reason the bidisperse (random-pore) diffusion model exists.

The bookkeeping identity that closes the problem is simply that the pellet is macro-void plus micro-void plus solid:

εM+εμ+εs=1

Watch the basis at every step. Macropore volume is quoted per pellet; micropore volume is quoted per gram of particles. Mixing the two is the classic way to lose all four marks.

Given

Ni/Al2O3 hydrogenation catalyst; 100–150 mesh alumina particles soaked in aqueous Ni(NO3)2, dried and reduced, ~7 wt% NiO; pelleted into cylinders.
Mass of pellet m = 3.15 g  ·  Diameter = 1.00 in.  ·  Thickness = 1/4 in.  ·  Volume = 3.22 cm3
Macropore volume of the pellet = 0.645 cm3  ·  Micropore volume = 0.40 cm3/g of particles

(a) Density of the pellet

ρP=3.153.22=0.978 g/cm3

(b) Macropore volume per gram

(Vg)M =0.6453.15=0.205 cm3/g

(c) Macropore void fraction of the pellet

Apply Eq. (8-17) on the pellet basis — 1 g of pellet occupies 1/ρP cm3:

εM= (Vg)M1/ρP =0.205×0.978=0.200

(d) Micropore void fraction of the pellet

εμ= (Vg)μ1/ρP =0.40×0.978=0.391

This works because the pellet is made entirely of the particles: 1 g of pellet contains 1 g of particles, so 0.40 cm3/g of particles is also 0.40 cm3 per gram of pellet.

(e) Solid fraction

εs=1−εM−εμ =1−0.200−0.391=0.409

(f) Density of the particles

Correct the pellet volume for the macropore volume — what is left is the volume actually occupied by particles:

ρp= 3.153.22−0.645 =3.152.575=1.22 g/cm3

or on a 1 g basis, ρp = ρP/[1 − (Vg)MρP] = 0.978/[1 − 0.205(0.978)] = 1.22 g/cm3

(g) Density of the solid phase

ρs= mass of pellet(volume of pellet)εs =ρPεs =0.9780.409=2.39 g/cm3

(h) Void fraction of the particles

εp= (Vg)μ1/ρp =ρp(Vg)μ =1.22×0.40=0.49

Note the difference between (d) and (h): εμ = 0.391 is micropore volume as a fraction of the pellet, while εp = 0.49 is micropore volume as a fraction of the particles alone. Both are “micropore porosity”; they differ only in the denominator. Confusing them is the single most common error in this example.

Why this matters downstream

  • εM and εμ feed straight into the random-pore (Wakao–Smith) effective-diffusivity model, De = DMεM2 + εμ2(1+3εM)Dμ/(1−εM), which you will use for effectiveness factors.
  • ρP converts an intrinsic rate per gram of catalyst into a rate per unit reactor volume — the bridge between the kinetics and the design equation.
  • ρs = 2.39 g/cm3 is a check on your arithmetic: it should come out near the true density of γ-alumina (~3.2 g/cm3) but lower here because of the 7 wt% NiO and residual closed porosity. A value far above the crystal density means you have made an error.
