Q.1 Explain the working principle of the measurement of surface area using the BET. [2] · BL 1,2

Principle

BET determines surface area from the multilayer physical adsorption (physisorption) of an inert gas on the catalyst surface. The standard adsorbate is nitrogen at its normal boiling point, −195.8 °C (77.4 K), maintained by a liquid-N2 bath. Weak van der Waals forces condense the gas onto the surface; from the isotherm one extracts the gas volume needed to complete exactly one monolayer, Vm. Knowing the cross-sectional area α of a single adsorbed molecule, the total surface area follows directly.

Assumptions (BET = Langmuir extended to multilayers)

  1. Adsorption is physical and occurs on a homogeneous surface of identical sites.
  2. Adsorption is multilayer — an infinite number of layers may form, and a layer need not be complete before the next begins.
  3. Langmuir kinetics apply to each layer separately (rate of adsorption on layer i = rate of desorption from layer i+1).
  4. The heat of adsorption of the first layer is E1; every layer above the first has heat of adsorption equal to the heat of liquefaction EL, i.e. they behave like bulk liquid.
  5. No lateral interaction between adsorbed molecules.

BET equation (linear form)

PV (P0 − P) = 1Vm C + C − 1Vm C · PP0 C ≈ exp[(E1 − EL)/RT]  ·  P0 = saturation vapour pressure of N2 at 77 K

Experimental procedure

  • The sample is degassed (heated under vacuum) to strip adsorbed moisture and impurities from the surface.
  • It is cooled in liquid nitrogen, and measured doses of N2 are admitted volumetrically.
  • The volume adsorbed V is recorded at several relative pressures P/P0.
  • Plot P/[V(P0 − P)] against P/P0. The plot is linear over P/P0 = 0.05 – 0.35.

Extracting the answer from the plot

slope s = C − 1VmC,   intercept i = 1VmC  ⇒  Vm = 1/(s + i),   C = 1 + s/i
Sg = Vm22414 × NA × α Vm in cm³(STP)/g · 22414 cm³/mol · NA = 6.022×1023 · αN2 = 16.2×10−16 cm²

In one line: measure how much N2 it takes to just cover the surface once, convert that to a number of molecules, and multiply by the footprint of one molecule.

Q.3 Two spherical catalysts — A: dp = 50 µm, ρp = 1.5, εp = 0.47; B: dp = 100 µm, ρp = 2, εp = 0.38. Determine which catalyst has higher surface area per unit mass. [2] · BL 3

Q.4 Explain the role of catalyst promoter with an example. [1] · BL 1

Definition

A promoter is a substance added in small quantity to a catalyst which itself possesses little or no catalytic activity, but which appreciably increases the activity, selectivity, or stability/life of the main catalyst.

Two roles

  • Structural (textural) promoter — physically separates the active crystallites, preventing sintering and crystal growth at reaction temperature, thereby preserving active surface area over the catalyst’s life.
  • Electronic promoter — alters the electron density of the active sites, tuning the strength of chemisorption of reactants and products, and hence the rate and selectivity.

Example — ammonia synthesis (Haber process) over Fe

  • Al2O3 (≈ 3%) — structural promoter: it spaces out the iron crystallites and stops them sintering at ~450 °C and 200 atm, keeping the surface area high for years of operation.
  • K2O (≈ 1%) — electronic promoter: it donates electron density to Fe, strengthening back-donation into the N≡N bond and thus assisting the rate-determining dissociative chemisorption of N2, while weakening NH3 binding so the product desorbs readily.
One-line answer A promoter is an essentially inactive additive that enhances a catalyst’s activity, selectivity or stability — e.g. Al2O3 (anti-sintering) and K2O (electronic) on the Fe catalyst used for NH3 synthesis.
Q.5 Explain Langmuir isotherm with expression. [2] · BL 2

Assumptions

  1. Adsorption is monolayer only — molecules do not stack on one another.
  2. The surface carries a fixed number of identical, energetically uniform sites.
  3. One molecule occupies one site; the heat of adsorption is independent of coverage.
  4. No interaction between molecules adsorbed on neighbouring sites.
  5. Adsorption is a dynamic equilibrium between adsorption and desorption.

Derivation

Let θ be the fraction of sites occupied, so (1 − θ) is the fraction vacant:

rads = ka P (1 − θ)      rdes = kd θ

At equilibrium rads = rdes, i.e. kaP(1 − θ) = kdθ. Rearranging with K = ka/kd:

θ = K P1 + K P Langmuir adsorption isotherm · K = adsorption equilibrium constant

In terms of adsorbed volume (θ = V/Vm)

V = Vm K P1 + K P   ⇒   PV = 1VmK + PVm Linearised form: plot P/V vs P → slope = 1/Vm, intercept = 1/(VmK)

Limiting behaviour

  • Low pressure (KP « 1): θ ≈ KP — coverage proportional to P, the reaction appears first order.
  • High pressure (KP » 1): θ → 1 — the surface is saturated and the reaction becomes zero order in that species.

Useful variants

  • Dissociative adsorption (A2 → 2A, requiring two sites): θ = √(KP) / (1 + √(KP)).
  • Competitive adsorption of several species: θi = KiPi / (1 + ΣKjPj) — the basis of all LHHW rate laws.
Limitation → link back to Q.1 Being restricted to a single layer, the Langmuir isotherm fails at high P/P0 where multilayer condensation sets in. Relaxing assumption 1 to allow infinite layers — with heat of adsorption equal to the heat of liquefaction beyond the first layer — gives the BET isotherm used for surface-area measurement.
