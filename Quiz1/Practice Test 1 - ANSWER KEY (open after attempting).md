## Section A — rapid fire (1 mark each)

**A1.** The **global** rate. It lumps all seven steps, so it depends on pellet size through the external and internal transport resistances; the intrinsic rate is a property of the surface chemistry only (steps 3–5) and is unchanged by crushing.

**A2.** Mercury **θ ≈ 140°** (non-wetting, must be *forced* in); nitrogen **θ = 0°** (wets the surface completely, condenses in the pores). Nitrogen wets.

**A3.** Physical adsorption: **< ~5 kcal/mol** (order of a heat of condensation). Chemisorption: **5–100 kcal/mol** (order of a heat of reaction).

**A4.** **100 Å** radius is the micropore/macropore boundary (Table 8-3). Essentially all the surface area lives in the **micropores**.

**A5.** vₘ = **1/(I + s)**; c = **s/I + 1** (equivalently I = 1/(vₘc), s = (c−1)/(vₘc)).

**A6.** α = **16.2 Å² = 16.2 × 10⁻¹⁶ cm²**; Sg = **4.35 × 10⁴ × vₘ**, with vₘ in cm³(STP)/g giving Sg in **cm²/g**.

**A7.** Helium → **solid (true) density ρs** (helium penetrates the pores, so it displaces only the skeleton). Mercury → **particle density ρp**. The mercury measurement needs the pores empty, and they stay empty because mercury does **not wet** the surface and at atmospheric pressure cannot enter pores (needs thousands of psi).

**A8.** Roughly **30% pore volume, 30% solid, 40% interparticle void**.

**A9.** (i) Nickel — **conductor (metal)** — hydrogenation / dehydrogenation. (ii) NiO, ZnO, Cu₂O — **semiconductor** — oxidation, desulfurization, hydrogenation-type electron transfer. (iii) Alumina, silica–alumina — **insulator** — dehydration, cracking, isomerization, polymerization (acid-site chemistry).

**A10.** A **carrier/support** is a high-area solid on which a small amount of expensive active material (Pt, Ni, Ag) is dispersed; it buys **large active surface area per gram of active agent** (and thermal stability against sintering).

## Section B — reasoning (3 marks each)

**B1.** 1 External (interphase) transport of reactant to the outer surface (**P**); 2 Intraparticle transport into the pores (**P**); 3 Adsorption on interior sites (**C**); 4 Surface reaction (**C**); 5 Desorption of product (**C**); 6 Intraparticle transport of product out (**P**); 7 External transport of product to the bulk (**P**). For a **nonporous** catalyst steps **2 and 6 disappear**. The intrinsic rate is **steps 3, 4, 5**.

**B2.** Set the two equal at steady state: kₘaₘ(C_b − C_s) = k aₘ C_s → **C_s = kₘC_b/(kₘ + k)** (Eq. 7-3). Substituting: **r_p = aₘC_b/(1/k + 1/kₘ)** (Eq. 7-4) — the two resistances **1/k (chemical) and 1/kₘ (mass transfer) add in series**, exactly like electrical resistances.
Fig. 7-1 regimes: **reaction control** (k ≪ kₘ): C_s ≈ C_b, profile flat, chemical step limits. **Diffusion control** (kₘ ≪ k): C_s → 0, profile falls steeply to zero at the surface, transport limits. **Intermediate**: 0 < C_s < C_b, sloping profile, both resistances matter.

**B3.** (i) *Temperature*: physisorption only at/below the condensation temperature; chemisorption possible far above it. (ii) *Heat*: < 5 vs 5–100 kcal/mol. (iii) *Reversibility*: physisorption fully and rapidly reversible with low activation energy (≤ ~1 kcal/mol); chemisorption often irreversible/activated. (iv) *Specificity and coverage*: physisorption is non-specific and multilayer — it covers **all** the solid; chemisorption is specific and **monolayer only**, on active centres.
Therefore **physisorption (N₂ BET) measures total surface area**, and **chemisorption (e.g. H₂ on Ni) measures active-centre area** — you need the non-specific one to count all the surface and the specific one to count only the catalytic sites.

**B4.** Assumptions: (1) all surface has the **same activity** for adsorption (energetically uniform); (2) **no interaction** between adsorbed molecules; (3) all adsorption occurs by the **same mechanism**, giving the same complex; (4) coverage is at most a **monolayer**.
Limits: Kp ≪ 1 → **θ ≈ Kp**, linear (Henry's law) — **first order** in the adsorbing species. Kp ≫ 1 → **θ → 1**, saturated monolayer — **zero order**.

**B5.** Raising velocity raises kₘ, which shrinks the external **concentration** difference (C_s rises toward C_b → rate up) but also raises the heat-transfer coefficient, which **cools the surface** back toward bulk temperature — and for an exothermic reaction a cooler surface means a **lower** rate. The two effects oppose; the net direction depends on which resistance dominates.
Cases: **SO₂ oxidation on Pt** — mass-transfer dominated, ignoring the concentration drop inflates the rate ~350% (factor 4.6), the 15 °C surface heating is worth ~31% the other way. **H₂ oxidation on Pt/alumina (Maymo & Smith)** — thermal dominated: no concentration difference, but ignoring ΔT is 20% low. **UO₂ hydrofluorination** — thermal wins strongly: the 85 °C rise gives +38% net while the 1.12/1.38 concentration drop alone would cost 19%.

**B6.** Pelleting pressure closes the space **between** particles — the **macropores** — whose volume collapses (1.08 → 0.265 cm³/g). It does not crush the particles themselves, so **micropore** volume barely changes (≈0.56 → 0.49), and since essentially **all the area is in the micropores**, Sg is untouched. Reactor consequence: the **active sites survive but the transport highways feeding them are narrowed** — larger intraparticle diffusion resistance, so the global rate can fall even though the intrinsic activity per gram is unchanged.

**B7.** **Promoter**: added *during preparation*, itself of little activity, improves activity/selectivity or stabilizes the catalyst (e.g. Al₂O₃/K₂O in ammonia-synthesis iron — prevents loss of area by sintering). **Inhibitor**: an anti-promoter, added deliberately in manufacture to *lower* an unwanted activity and so raise selectivity (halogen compounds on Ag for ethylene oxide). **Poison**: arrives *during operation*, in the feed or as a product, and destroys activity (coke, sulfur compounds).
5 wt% vs 0.5 wt% NiO: the concentrated solution deposits **larger nickel particles** (atoms stacked on one another), so less active surface despite more metal — 5.0 wt% built up as **ten separate 0.5 wt% depositions was 11× more active** than the same amount added at once. What matters is **dispersion** (active surface area per gram of metal), not weight percent.

## Section C — numerical

### C1 (4 marks)
dₚ = 60 µm = 6 × 10⁻³ cm, ρₚ = 1.4 g/cm³, using **Sg = 6/(ρₚdₚ)**, area/volume = 6/dₚ.
- (a) 6/dₚ = 6/(6 × 10⁻³) = **1000 cm²/cm³ = 1 × 10⁵ m²/m³**
- (b) Sg = 6/(1.4 × 6 × 10⁻³) = 714 cm²/g = **0.071 m²/g**
- (c) dₚ = 6/(ρₚSg) = 6/(1.4 × 250 × 10⁴ cm²/g) = **1.7 × 10⁻⁶ cm ≈ 171 Å (0.017 µm)**
- (d) A molecular-scale particle — impossible to handle. So the area of a real catalyst is **internal, in the pores**, not external. (External area of a normal pellet is negligible compared with Sg.)

### C2 (5 marks)
Linearise as **p/q = 1/(q_max K) + p/q_max**. Plot p/q vs p:
p/q = 0.120, 0.200, 0.300, 0.500, 0.901 at p = 0.1, 0.5, 1, 2, 4.
Slope ≈ **0.200 g/mmol**, intercept ≈ **0.100 g·atm/mmol**.
- q_max = 1/slope = **5.0 mmol/g**
- K = slope/intercept = **2.0 atm⁻¹**
(Equivalent linearisations 1/q vs 1/p, or q vs q/p, are acceptable if consistent.)

### C3 (6 marks)
- (a) vₘ(total) = 1/(I+s) = 1/(15.3 × 10⁻³) = 65.4 cm³ STP; per gram = 65.4/0.85 = **76.9 cm³(STP)/g**
- (b) molecules/g = (76.9/22,400) × 6.02 × 10²³ = 2.07 × 10²¹; Sg = 2.07 × 10²¹ × 16.2 × 10⁻¹⁶ = 3.35 × 10⁶ cm²/g = **335 m²/g**. Shortcut: 4.35 × 10⁴ × 76.9 = 3.35 × 10⁶ cm²/g ✓
- (c) c = s/I + 1 = 15.0/0.30 + 1 = **51**. Large c ⇒ the first layer is bound **much more strongly** than subsequent layers condense, so the monolayer completes cleanly before multilayers start — a sharp knee and a well-defined vₘ. (As c → 1 the knee vanishes and vₘ becomes ill-defined.)
- (d) Same vₘ (same monolayer), only α changes: Sg = 335 × 14.2/16.2 = **≈293 m²/g**. The two should agree within experimental error; the difference is the assumed molecular area, not real physics.

### C4 (7 marks)
- (a) ρs = 50.0/20.0 = **2.50 g/cm³**
- (b) ρₚ = 50.0/42.0 = **1.19 g/cm³**
- (c) V_g = (42.0 − 20.0)/50.0 = **0.44 cm³/g**
- (d) ε_p = V_gρ_s/(V_gρ_s + 1) = 1.10/2.10 = **0.524**; ε_p = V_gρ_p = 0.44 × 1.19 = **0.524** ✓
- (e) ā = 2V_g/Sg = 2(0.44)/(200 × 10⁴ cm²/g) = 4.4 × 10⁻⁷ cm = **44 Å**
- (f) p = 8.75 × 10⁵/a(Å) = 8.75 × 10⁵/44 ≈ **2.0 × 10⁴ psi**
- (g) That is far beyond an ordinary porosimeter (~10,000 psi for 100 Å) and needs special high-pressure apparatus, so **nitrogen desorption** is the right instrument for this pore size. (Mercury porosimetry is for the macropores, > ~200 Å.)

*Trap check:* the Wheeler radius assumes a **monodisperse** pore system; if this catalyst were bidisperse, 44 Å could fall in an empty valley between the micro- and macropore peaks and mean nothing.

### C5 (3 marks)
p₀/p = 1/0.60 = 1.667. log(1.667) = 0.2218 → a − δ = 9.52/0.2218 = **42.9 Å**.
ln(1.667) = 0.5108; (0.5108)^(1/3) = 0.799 → δ = 7.34/0.799 = **9.2 Å**.
**a = 42.9 + 9.2 ≈ 52 Å.**
The correction is worth keeping: δ is **21%** of a — dropping it would report a 43 Å pore instead of 52 Å. (Watch the log vs ln: the first formula is base-10, the second natural.)
