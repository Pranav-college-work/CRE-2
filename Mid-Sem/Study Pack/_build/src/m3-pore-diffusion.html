Pore diffusion, Thiele modulus and the effectiveness factor

When reactant must diffuse into a porous pellet, the inside sees a lower concentration than the outside. The effectiveness factor measures the loss; the Thiele modulus predicts it.

OL 18 intro, 18.1, 18.2, 18.3HSF 15.1, 15.2, 15.3.1Lectures 1–11 Sep 2026
Exam focus, from the professor

Know: how to formulate the problem (balance, flux, rate law, boundary conditions, dimensionless form), what \(\eta\) and the Thiele modulus mean, and how to use them. Not the focus: solving the differential equation. Solutions are included below, marked "for understanding", so the \(\eta\) formulas are not a mystery.

1The spectrum of kinetic regimes (Levenspiel, Ch. 18 introduction)

Catalysts can speed a reaction a million-fold, but for mixtures such as petroleum the most important property is selectivity. Some general observations (OL): catalyst selection is still largely trial and error; copying a good catalyst's chemical composition does not guarantee activity (physical structure matters; heating above a critical temperature can destroy activity); a catalyst lowers the energy barrier but never changes the equilibrium; a large accessible surface is needed.

2§18.1 The rate equation for surface kinetics

The active-site theory views three successive steps at the surface: (1) a molecule adsorbs on an active site; (2) it reacts with a molecule on an adjacent site (dual site), with one from the gas (single site), or simply decomposes (single site); (3) products desorb and free the site. All species are assumed to be in equilibrium except in the controlling step. Every such rate law has the form

OL 18.1$$\text{rate of reaction} = \frac{(\text{kinetic term})(\text{driving force or displacement from equilibrium})}{(\text{resistance term})}$$

For \(A + B \rightleftharpoons R + S\) with an inert carrier U present:

$$\text{adsorption of A controls:}\quad -r''_A = \frac{k\,(p_A - p_Rp_S/Kp_B)}{\left(1 + K_Ap_Rp_S/Kp_B + K_Bp_B + K_Rp_R + K_Sp_S + K_Up_U\right)^2}$$
$$\text{surface reaction between adjacent adsorbed A and B controls:}\quad -r''_A = \frac{k\,(p_Ap_B - p_Rp_S/K)}{\left(1 + K_Ap_A + K_Bp_B + K_Rp_R + K_Sp_S + K_Up_U\right)^2}$$

These carry three to seven adjustable constants. Levenspiel then argues not to use them for design:

For: truth and predictability

If the true mechanism is found, extrapolation to new conditions is much safer.

Against: finding the mechanism

With 3–7 constants per model, you must show one family fits so much better that all others are rejected. Differences are usually within experimental error (Chou's analysis of 18 mechanisms for the codimer example). A good fit is only "one of good fit", not reality.

Against: combining resistances

Combining a multi-constant surface rate with pore or film diffusion becomes impractical. Replace it by an equivalent first-order expression.

Summary on surface kinetics

Most catalytic conversion data can be fitted adequately by simple first- or nth-order rate expressions (Prater and Lago). So use the simplest correlating expression, first- or nth-order kinetics, for the surface reaction. This also matches Fogler §10.3.7: at high temperature the Langmuir–Hinshelwood denominator tends to 1.

\(k'''\) (Levenspiel) or \(k_1 = k''_1S_a\rho_c\) (Fogler), units \(\mathrm{s^{-1}}\) for first order. If you are given \(k'\) per kg, multiply by the pellet density \(\rho_c\) first.

4§18.2 Pore diffusion with surface kinetics: a single cylindrical pore

Take one cylindrical pore of radius \(r\) and length \(L\). Reactant A diffuses in from the mouth, reacts on the walls by a first-order reaction, and product diffuses out.

$$A \rightarrow \text{product},\qquad -r''_A = -\frac{1}{S}\frac{dN_A}{dt} = k''C_A$$

Formulation

  1. Steady-state balance on the slice (output minus input plus disappearance by reaction equals zero): $$-\pi r^2\mathscr{D}\left(\frac{dC_A}{dx}\right)_{out} + \pi r^2\mathscr{D}\left(\frac{dC_A}{dx}\right)_{in} + k''C_A\,(2\pi r\,\Delta x) = 0$$
  2. Divide by \(\pi r^2\mathscr{D}\Delta x\) and let \(\Delta x\to0\): $$\frac{d^2C_A}{dx^2} - \frac{2k''}{\mathscr{D}r}C_A = 0\qquad(\text{OL eq. 3})$$
  3. Change basis to volume (OL eqs 4, 5): \(kV = k''S\), so for the pore $$k = k''\left(\frac{\text{surface}}{\text{volume}}\right) = k''\left(\frac{2\pi rL}{\pi r^2L}\right) = \frac{2k''}{r}\qquad\Rightarrow\qquad \frac{d^2C_A}{dx^2} - \frac{k}{\mathscr{D}}C_A = 0\quad(6)$$
  4. Boundary conditions, from the physics of the pore: $$\text{pore entrance: } C_A = C_{As}\ \text{at}\ x=0\quad(8a)\qquad\text{sealed end, no flux: } \frac{dC_A}{dx} = 0\ \text{at}\ x = L\quad(8b)$$
  5. Define the parameter that appears: $$m = \sqrt{\frac{k}{\mathscr{D}}} = \sqrt{\frac{2k''}{\mathscr{D}r}},\qquad mL = \text{Thiele modulus } M_T$$
For understanding: solving the single-pore equation

General solution (OL eq. 7): \(C_A = M_1e^{mx} + M_2e^{-mx}\). The boundary conditions give (eq. 9)

$$M_1 = \frac{C_{As}e^{-mL}}{e^{mL}+e^{-mL}},\qquad M_2 = \frac{C_{As}e^{mL}}{e^{mL}+e^{-mL}}\qquad\Rightarrow\qquad \frac{C_A}{C_{As}} = \frac{e^{m(L-x)} + e^{-m(L-x)}}{e^{mL}+e^{-mL}} = \frac{\cosh m(L-x)}{\cosh mL}\quad(10)$$

Average the rate over the pore (first order, so the rate is proportional to concentration):

$$\eta = \frac{\bar C_A}{C_{As}} = \frac{1}{L}\int_0^L\frac{\cosh m(L-x)}{\cosh mL}\,dx = \frac{\tanh mL}{mL}$$

The effectiveness factor

OL 11, 12$$\eta = \frac{(\text{actual mean reaction rate within pore})}{(\text{rate if not slowed by pore diffusion})} = \frac{\bar r_{A,\ \text{with diffusion}}}{r_{A,\ \text{without diffusion resistance}}},\qquad \eta_{\text{first order}} = \frac{\bar C_A}{C_{As}} = \frac{\tanh mL}{mL}$$
Small \(mL\) (\(mL < 0.4\))

\(\eta\approx1\). Concentration barely drops; pore diffusion offers negligible resistance. A small \(mL\) means a short pore, slow reaction or rapid diffusion.

Large \(mL\) (\(mL > 4\))

\(\eta\approx1/mL\). Concentration drops to zero close to the mouth. Strong pore resistance: diffusion strongly influences the rate.

Volume change on reaction (OL Fig. 18.5)

If the reaction expands the gas (\(\varepsilon_A>0\)), extra outflow from the pore makes it harder for reactant to diffuse in, lowering \(\eta\). Contraction pulls fluid in and raises \(\eta\). For first order, Thiele found this simply shifts the \(\eta\) vs \(M_T\) curve.

5§18.3 Porous catalyst particles

The single-pore result approximates particles of any shape once three things are replaced:

  1. Diffusion coefficient. Replace the molecular diffusivity \(\mathscr{D}\) by the effective diffusivity \(\mathscr{D}_e\) in the porous structure (values in Weisz 1959; see §7 below for Fogler's estimate).
  2. Particle size. Use the characteristic length $$L = \left(\frac{\text{volume of particle}}{\text{exterior surface available for reactant penetration}}\right) = \begin{cases}\dfrac{\text{thickness}}{2} & \text{flat plate (both faces)}\\[2mm] \dfrac{R}{2} & \text{cylinder}\\[2mm] \dfrac{R}{3} & \text{sphere}\end{cases}$$
  3. Rate measure. Use \(-r'''_A = k'''C_A\) per volume of particle.

Item 4 · \(\eta\) for three shapes (Thiele 1939, Aris 1957)

OL 20–23$$A\rightarrow R,\quad -r'''_A = k'''C_A\,\eta,\qquad \eta = \begin{cases}\dfrac{1}{M_T}\tanh M_T & \text{flat plate}\\[3mm] \dfrac{1}{M_T}\,\dfrac{I_1(2M_T)}{I_0(2M_T)} & \text{cylinder (Bessel functions)}\\[3mm] \dfrac{1}{M_T}\left(\dfrac{1}{\tanh 3M_T} - \dfrac{1}{3M_T}\right) & \text{sphere}\end{cases}\qquad M_T = L\sqrt{\frac{k'''}{\mathscr{D}_e}}$$

With \(L\) defined as volume over external area, all three curves nearly coincide. If you know \(\mathscr{D}_e\), \(k'''\) and \(L\), find \(M_T\) and read \(\eta\).

Item 5 · Finding pore resistance from experiment: the Wagner–Weisz–Wheeler modulus

\(M_T\) needs \(k'''\), which is what you are trying to measure. The trick is a modulus built only from observable quantities. Since \(-r'''_{A,obs} = \eta\,k'''C_{As}\):

OL 24$$M_W = M_T^2\,\eta = L^2\,\frac{k'''\eta\,C_{As}}{\mathscr{D}_eC_{As}} = L^2\,\frac{(-r'''_A/C_A)_{obs}}{\mathscr{D}_e}$$

Item 7 · Particles of different sizes

Compare particles of size \(R_1\) and \(R_2\) (same catalyst, same \(C_A\)):

OL 25$$\text{diffusion-free:}\quad \frac{r'_{A1}}{r'_{A2}} = \frac{\eta_1k'C_A}{\eta_2k'C_A} = \frac{\eta_1}{\eta_2} = 1\qquad\qquad \text{strong resistance:}\quad \frac{r'_{A1}}{r'_{A2}} = \frac{\eta_1}{\eta_2} = \frac{M_{T2}}{M_{T1}} = \frac{R_2}{R_1}$$

In the strong-resistance regime the rate varies inversely with particle size. This is the standard experimental test: crush the catalyst; if the rate per mass does not change, there is no pore resistance.

Levenspiel's summary and the best particle size (OL eqs 34, 35, p. 391)

Levenspiel closes §18.3 by collecting the first-order results in one place:

OL 34$$-r'''_A = k'''C_{As}\,\mathscr{E},\qquad \mathscr{E} = \frac{\tanh M_T}{M_T},\qquad M_T = L\sqrt{\frac{k'''}{\mathscr{D}_e}},\qquad M_W = M_T^2\,\mathscr{E} = \frac{L^2\,(-r'''_{A,obs})}{C_{A,obs}\,\mathscr{D}_e}$$

\(k'''C_{As}\)

The rate without any diffusion effects. \(\mathscr{E} = 1\) means no diffusion resistance.

\(\mathscr{E}\), effectiveness factor

A "fudge factor" between 0 and 1 that accounts for the resistance to pore diffusion. The flat-plate expression \(\tanh M_T/M_T\) is a good approximation for all particle shapes.

\(L\) and \(\mathscr{D}_e\)

\(L\): characteristic length (particle volume over external area). \(\mathscr{D}_e\): effective diffusion coefficient in the porous solid, \(\mathrm{m^3\ gas/(m\ solid\cdot s)}\).

\(M_T\), Thiele modulus

Useful for predicting reactor behaviour from known kinetic information, that is, a known \(k'''\).

\(M_W\), Weisz modulus

Useful for interpreting experiments, since it contains only observables.

How to use them

Evaluate \(M_T\) or \(M_W\), find \(\mathscr{E}\) from the equations or figures, and put \(\mathscr{E}\) into the rate equation.

Desirable processing range (OL eq. 35)

Fine solids are free of pore-diffusion resistance but are difficult to use: imagine the pressure drop of a packed bed of face powder. A bed of large particles has a small \(\Delta p\) but is liable to be in the regime of strong pore diffusion, where much of each pellet's interior is unused. For the most effective operation, use the largest particle size that is still free of diffusional resistance:

$$M_T \cong 0.4\qquad\text{or}\qquad M_W \cong 0.15$$

7Effective diffusivity (HSF 15.1, 15.2.1)

Homogeneous systems first (15.1). One-dimensional steady diffusion with reaction, dilute, through a stagnant film:

$$-\frac{dW_{Az}}{dz} + r_A = 0,\qquad W_{Az} = -D_{AB}\frac{dC_A}{dz}\qquad\Rightarrow\qquad D_{AB}\frac{d^2C_A}{dz^2} + r_A = 0$$

In a pellet, pores are not straight cylinders but tortuous, interconnected paths of pore bodies and throats. Rather than model each path, define an effective diffusivity that describes the average diffusion at a radial position, with the flux based on the total area (voids and solids) normal to transport, \(4\pi r^2\).

15-1$$D_e = \frac{D_{AB}\,\varphi_p\,\sigma_c}{\tilde\tau}$$

\(\varphi_p\) pellet porosity

$$\varphi_p = \frac{\text{volume of void space}}{\text{total volume (voids + solids)}}$$

Not all of the area normal to the flux is open.

\(\tilde\tau\) tortuosity

$$\tilde\tau = \frac{\text{actual distance travelled between two points}}{\text{shortest distance between them}}$$

Paths are tortuous. Typical 3; values of 6–10 are common.

\(\sigma_c\) constriction factor

Pores vary in cross-section. Depends on the ratio of maximum to minimum pore area: 1 when \(A_1 = A_2\), about 0.5 when \(A_1/A_2 = 10\). Some authors lump \(\tilde\tau/\sigma_c\) into one "tortuosity factor".

Example 15-1 · typical effective diffusivity

With \(\sigma_c = 0.8\), \(\tilde\tau = 3.0\), \(\varphi_p = 0.40\) and \(D_{AB} = 10^{-6}\) \(\mathrm{m^{2}/s}\):

$$D_e = \frac{(0.40)(0.8)}{3.0}D_{AB} = 0.106\,D_{AB}\approx 0.1\times10^{-6} = 10^{-7}\ \mathrm{m^2/s}$$

A representative effective diffusivity is about 10 % of the gas-phase value. The same equation applies with the Knudsen diffusivity \(D_K\) in place of \(D_{AB}\) for very small pores.

8Formulating diffusion and reaction in a spherical pellet (HSF 15.2.2, 15.2.3)

Irreversible isomerisation \(A\rightarrow B\) on the pore walls inside a sphere of radius \(R\). Equimolar counter-diffusion, constant \(T\) and \(P\).

Step by step

  1. Mole balance on the shell (in minus out plus generation equals zero): $$W_{Ar}\cdot4\pi r^2\big|_r - W_{Ar}\cdot4\pi r^2\big|_{r+\Delta r} + r'_A\rho_c\cdot4\pi r_m^2\Delta r = 0\quad(15\text{-}5)$$
  2. Divide by \(-4\pi\Delta r\), let \(\Delta r\to0\): $$\frac{d(W_{Ar}r^2)}{dr} - r'_A\rho_cr^2 = 0\quad(15\text{-}6)$$
  3. Flux law, equimolar counter-diffusion (or dilute), \(C_A\) per volume of gas in the pores: $$W_{Ar} = -D_e\frac{dC_A}{dr}\quad(15\text{-}7)$$
  4. Substitute: $$\frac{d\left[-D_e\,(dC_A/dr)\,r^2\right]}{dr} - r^2\rho_cr'_A = 0\quad(15\text{-}8)$$
  5. Rate law. Surface reaction of nth order in gas concentration (reasonable at high \(T\), §10.3.7): \(-r''_A = k''_nC_A^n\), so \(-r'_A\rho_c = k''_nS_a\rho_cC_A^n = k_nC_A^n\): $$\frac{d\left[r^2D_e\,dC_A/dr\right]}{dr} - r^2\,\underbrace{k''_nS_a\rho_c}_{k_n}\,C_A^n = 0\quad(15\text{-}10)$$
  6. Constant \(D_e\): expand and divide by \(r^2D_e\): $$\boxed{\frac{d^2C_A}{dr^2} + \frac{2}{r}\frac{dC_A}{dr} - \frac{k_n}{D_e}C_A^n = 0}\quad(15\text{-}11)$$
  7. Boundary conditions: $$C_A \text{ is finite at } r = 0\ \ (\text{equivalently } dC_A/dr = 0 \text{ by symmetry}),\qquad C_A = C_{As}\text{ at } r = R$$

Dimensionless form and the Thiele modulus (15.2.3)

15-12, 15-13$$\psi = \frac{C_A}{C_{As}},\qquad \lambda = \frac{r}{R}\qquad\Rightarrow\qquad \psi = 1\text{ at }\lambda = 1,\qquad \psi\text{ finite at }\lambda = 0$$

Chain rule: \(\dfrac{dC_A}{dr} = \dfrac{dC_A}{d\psi}\dfrac{d\psi}{d\lambda}\dfrac{d\lambda}{dr} = \dfrac{C_{As}}{R}\dfrac{d\psi}{d\lambda}\) and \(\dfrac{d^2C_A}{dr^2} = \dfrac{C_{As}}{R^2}\dfrac{d^2\psi}{d\lambda^2}\). The flux and the total rate of consumption in the pellet:

15-16, 15-17$$W_{Ar} = -\frac{D_eC_{As}}{R}\frac{d\psi}{d\lambda},\qquad M_A = -4\pi R^2W_{Ar}\big|_{r=R} = 4\pi RD_eC_{As}\left.\frac{d\psi}{d\lambda}\right|_{\lambda=1}$$

Everything that diffuses in at the surface reacts inside (Fogler: "a black hole"), so \(M_A\) is the overall rate. Dividing eq. 15-11 by \(C_{As}/R^2\):

15-19, 15-20$$\boxed{\frac{d^2\psi}{d\lambda^2} + \frac{2}{\lambda}\frac{d\psi}{d\lambda} - \phi_n^2\psi^n = 0},\qquad \phi_n^2 = \frac{k_nR^2C_{As}^{\,n-1}}{D_e} = \frac{k_nRC_{As}^n}{D_e\left[(C_{As}-0)/R\right]} = \frac{\text{“a” surface reaction rate}}{\text{“a” diffusion rate}}$$

The Thiele modulus \(\phi_n\) always carries the order subscript (to distinguish it from porosity \(\phi\)). Large \(\phi_n\): internal diffusion limits. Small \(\phi_n\): surface reaction limits.

First order (weakly adsorbed A and B, surface reaction limiting): \(-r_A = k_1C_A\), \(k_1 = S_a\rho_ck''_1\) (15-21):

15-22, 15-23$$\frac{d^2\psi}{d\lambda^2} + \frac{2}{\lambda}\frac{d\psi}{d\lambda} - \phi_1^2\psi = 0,\qquad \phi_1 = R\sqrt{\frac{k''_1\rho_cS_a}{D_e}} = R\sqrt{\frac{k_1}{D_e}}\qquad\left[\mathrm{m}\left(\frac{\mathrm{s^{-1}}}{\mathrm{m^2/s}}\right)^{1/2} = \text{dimensionless}\right]$$
For understanding: solving for the first-order profile (15.2.4)

Substitute \(y = \psi\lambda\). Then \(\dfrac{d\psi}{d\lambda} = \dfrac{1}{\lambda}\dfrac{dy}{d\lambda} - \dfrac{y}{\lambda^2}\) and \(\dfrac{d^2\psi}{d\lambda^2} = \dfrac{1}{\lambda}\dfrac{d^2y}{d\lambda^2} - \dfrac{2}{\lambda^2}\dfrac{dy}{d\lambda} + \dfrac{2y}{\lambda^3}\), and eq. 15-22 becomes

$$\frac{d^2y}{d\lambda^2} - \phi_1^2y = 0\quad(15\text{-}26)\qquad\Rightarrow\qquad \psi = \frac{A_1}{\lambda}\cosh\phi_1\lambda + \frac{B_1}{\lambda}\sinh\phi_1\lambda$$

As \(\lambda\to0\), \(\cosh\phi_1\lambda\to1\) and \(1/\lambda\to\infty\), so finiteness at the centre forces \(A_1 = 0\). \(\psi(1) = 1\) gives \(B_1 = 1/\sinh\phi_1\):

$$\psi = \frac{C_A}{C_{As}} = \frac{1}{\lambda}\,\frac{\sinh\phi_1\lambda}{\sinh\phi_1}\quad(15\text{-}27)$$

Small \(\phi_1\): shallow profile, whole interior accessible (surface reaction controls). Large \(\phi_1\): reactant consumed near the outside — if expensive Pt is used, plate it only near the surface (egg-shell catalyst).

9The internal effectiveness factor (HSF 15.3.1)

15-28$$\eta = \frac{\text{actual overall rate of reaction}}{\text{rate that would result if the entire interior surface were exposed to the external surface conditions } C_{As},\,T_s}$$

Because the rate per volume, per mass and per area differ only by constant factors,

$$\eta = \frac{-r_A}{-r_{As}} = \frac{-r'_A}{-r'_{As}} = \frac{-r''_A}{-r''_{As}} = \frac{-r_A\times V_p}{-r_{As}\times V_p} = \frac{M_A}{M_{As}},\qquad 0<\eta\le1$$

The overall rate \(-r'_A\) is the observed rate, \(-r_{A(obs)}\).

Derivation for first order

  1. Denominator, whole pellet at surface conditions: $$M_{As} = -r_{As}\cdot\frac43\pi R^3 = k_1C_{As}\cdot\frac43\pi R^3\quad(15\text{-}29)$$
  2. Numerator, everything that diffuses in reacts, eq. 15-17 with the slope of 15-27 at the surface: $$\left.\frac{d\psi}{d\lambda}\right|_{\lambda=1} = \left[\frac{\phi_1\cosh\lambda\phi_1}{\lambda\sinh\phi_1} - \frac{1}{\lambda^2}\frac{\sinh\lambda\phi_1}{\sinh\phi_1}\right]_{\lambda=1} = \phi_1\coth\phi_1 - 1\quad(15\text{-}30)$$ $$M_A = 4\pi RD_eC_{As}\,(\phi_1\coth\phi_1 - 1)\quad(15\text{-}31)$$
  3. Ratio: $$\eta = \frac{M_A}{M_{As}} = \frac{4\pi RD_eC_{As}(\phi_1\coth\phi_1-1)}{k_1C_{As}\frac43\pi R^3} = \frac{3}{k_1R^2/D_e}(\phi_1\coth\phi_1 - 1)$$ $$\boxed{\eta = \frac{3}{\phi_1^2}\left(\phi_1\coth\phi_1 - 1\right)}\quad(15\text{-}32)$$

\(\phi_1\to0\)

\(\eta\to1\): surface-reaction-limited. Happens for very small particles.

\(\phi_1 > 2\)

$$\eta\approx\frac{3}{\phi_1^2}(\phi_1 - 1)$$

\(\phi_1 > 20\)

$$\eta\approx\frac{3}{\phi_1}$$

e.g. \(\phi_1 = 30\) gives \(\eta \approx 0.1\): internal-diffusion-limited.

Large Thiele modulus: the overall rate (HSF 15-33, 15-34, p. 733)

For large \(\phi_1\) the reaction is internal-diffusion-limited. When the reaction is either internal-surface-reaction-limited or internal-diffusion-limited, factors that influence external mass transport, such as fluid velocity, have a negligible effect on the overall rate.

15-33$$\eta \approx \frac{3}{\phi_1} = \frac{3}{R}\sqrt{\frac{D_e}{k_1}}$$
15-34$$-r_A = \left(\frac{\text{actual reaction rate}}{\text{reaction rate at } C_{As}}\right)\times(\text{reaction rate at } C_{As}) = \eta\,(-r_{As}) = \eta\,(k_1C_{As})$$
$$\boxed{-r_A = \frac{3}{R}\sqrt{D_e\,k_1}\;C_{As} = \frac{3}{R}\sqrt{D_eS_a\rho_ck''_1}\;C_{As}}$$
Fogler's margin note

For catalytic packed beds under internal diffusion limitation, the rate varies inversely with the particle diameter. It is Levenspiel's strong-resistance rule \(r_1/r_2 = R_2/R_1\) in Fogler's notation.

Same curve, two notations

Levenspiel's sphere \(L = R/3\), so \(M_T = \dfrac R3\sqrt{k'''/\mathscr{D}_e} = \phi_1/3\). Put \(\phi_1 = 3M_T\) into 15-32:

$$\frac{3}{9M_T^2}\left(3M_T\coth3M_T - 1\right) = \frac{1}{M_T}\left(\coth3M_T - \frac{1}{3M_T}\right)$$

which is exactly OL eq. 22. Strong resistance: \(3/\phi_1 = 1/M_T\). ✔

Fogler Fig. 15-5 also shows (a) spheres with zero, first and second order, and (b) first order in sphere, cylinder and slab with \(\phi_1\) based on \(R/3\), \(R/2\) and \(L\) — the same geometry collapse as OL Fig. 18.6. For \(\phi_s0 = R\sqrt{k_0/(D_eC_{A0})}\), \(\phi_{s1} = R\sqrt{k_1/D_e}\), \(\phi_{s2} = R\sqrt{k_2C_{A0}/D_e}\). Volume change (\(\varepsilon\ne0\)) needs the corrections of Fig. 15-6.

How to reduce internal diffusion limitation

Lower \(\phi\): use smaller pellets (smaller \(R\)), raise \(D_e\) (larger pores, higher porosity, lower tortuosity), or put the active metal only in an outer shell. Raising temperature makes it worse (\(k\) grows faster than \(D_e\)).

10Formulation recipe and notation map

Recipe: formulate any isothermal diffusion–reaction problem
  1. Geometry and element. Slab or pore: slice \(A\,\Delta x\). Cylinder: shell \(2\pi rL\,\Delta r\). Sphere: shell \(4\pi r^2\Delta r\).
  2. Mole balance: \(\text{in} - \text{out} + \text{generation} = 0\) (steady state).
  3. Flux law: \(W_A = -D_e\,dC_A/dr\) (Fick, effective diffusivity).
  4. Rate law on the pellet-volume basis: \(-r_A = k_nC_A^n\), \(k_n = \rho_cS_ak''_n\).
  5. Divide and take the limit to get the ODE; state the two boundary conditions (surface concentration known; symmetry or no-flux at the centre / sealed end).
  6. Nondimensionalise with \(\psi = C_A/C_{As}\) and \(\lambda = r/R\) (or \(x/L\)); the group left in front of \(\psi^n\) is \(\phi_n^2\).
  7. Define \(\eta\) as \(\dfrac{\text{flux in at the surface}\times\text{area}}{\text{volume}\times\text{rate at surface conditions}}\).

Slab (half-thickness L)

$$\frac{d^2C_A}{dx^2} - \frac{k}{D_e}C_A = 0$$

\(C_A = C_{As}\) at faces; \(dC_A/dx = 0\) at centre.

Cylinder (radius R)

$$\frac{1}{r}\frac{d}{dr}\left(r\frac{dC_A}{dr}\right) - \frac{k}{D_e}C_A = 0$$

\(C_A = C_{As}\) at \(r=R\); finite at \(r=0\).

Sphere (radius R)

$$\frac{1}{r^2}\frac{d}{dr}\left(r^2\frac{dC_A}{dr}\right) - \frac{k}{D_e}C_A = 0$$

\(C_A = C_{As}\) at \(r=R\); finite at \(r=0\).

11Self-test

Define the effectiveness factor and the Thiele modulus in words.

\(\eta\): actual overall rate in the pellet divided by the rate if the whole interior were at the external surface concentration and temperature. Thiele modulus: square root of the ratio of a characteristic surface reaction rate to a characteristic diffusion rate; it tells how far reactant penetrates before it reacts.

Write the balance and boundary conditions for first-order reaction in a flat catalyst slab of thickness \(2L\) sealed at the edges.
$$D_e\frac{d^2C_A}{dx^2} - k_1C_A = 0,\qquad C_A = C_{As}\ \text{at}\ x = \pm L\ (\text{or at } x = L),\qquad \frac{dC_A}{dx} = 0\ \text{at}\ x = 0$$

Dimensionless: \(\psi'' - \phi^2\psi = 0\), \(\phi = L\sqrt{k_1/D_e}\); \(\eta = \tanh\phi/\phi\).

6 mm spheres, \(k''' = 2\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}\), \(\mathscr{D}_e = 5\times10^{-7}\ \mathrm{m^2/s}\). Is pore diffusion important? (\(R = 3\) mm)
$$M_T = \frac{R}{3}\sqrt{\frac{k'''}{\mathscr{D}_e}} = 0.001\sqrt{\frac{2}{5\times10^{-7}}} = 2.0,\qquad \eta = \frac12\left(\frac{1}{\tanh6} - \frac16\right) = 0.417$$

Intermediate regime: the pellet works at 42 % of its potential. Fogler form: \(\phi_1 = 6\), \(\eta = \frac{3}{36}(6\coth6 - 1) = 0.417\). ✔

A reaction in 1 mm particles gives the same rate per gram as in 0.2 mm particles. Conclusion?

Rate independent of size: diffusion-free regime, \(\eta \approx 1\) for both. The measured rate is the intrinsic rate. (If it had fallen five-fold, strong pore resistance.)

True order 2, \(E_{true} = 150\) kJ/mol. What order and activation energy would you observe on large pellets at high temperature?

Order \((2+1)/2 = 1.5\). \(E_{obs} = (E_{true}+E_{diff})/2 \approx (150 + 5\text{ to }15)/2 \approx 78\)–\(83\) kJ/mol, roughly 75 kJ/mol if \(E_{diff}\) is neglected.

Why is \(M_W\) useful when \(M_T\) already exists?

\(M_T\) contains the intrinsic \(k'''\), unknown if the measurement was already slowed by diffusion. \(M_W = L^2(-r'''_A)_{obs}/(\mathscr{D}_eC_{As})\) uses only the observed rate, measured size, surface concentration and effective diffusivity, so it diagnoses the regime directly.

Why does the flux in the spherical shell balance use \(4\pi r^2\) (total area) and not the pore area?

Because \(D_e\) is defined to include the porosity (and tortuosity, constriction): \(D_e = D_{AB}\varphi_p\sigma_c/\tilde\tau\). The flux \(W_{Ar} = -D_e\,dC_A/dr\) is then per unit total area.

Estimate \(D_e\) for \(D_{AB} = 1\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{m^2/s}\), \(\varphi_p = 0.45\), \(\sigma_c = 0.8\), \(\tilde\tau = 3.5\).
$$D_e = \frac{(1\times10^{-5})(0.45)(0.8)}{3.5} = 1.03\times10^{-6}\ \mathrm{m^2/s}$$
Levenspiel recommends a "desirable processing range" for particle size. What is it, and why?

Use the largest particles that are still free of pore-diffusion resistance: \(M_T \cong 0.4\) or \(M_W \cong 0.15\) (OL eq. 35). Smaller particles use their whole interior but give a very large bed pressure drop; larger ones have a small \(\Delta p\) but leave much of each pellet's interior unused.

Under strong internal diffusion limitation, how does the rate depend on pellet diameter and on gas velocity?

\(-r_A = \dfrac{3}{R}\sqrt{D_ek_1}\,C_{As}\) (Fogler 15-34 with 15-33): inversely proportional to pellet size. Gas velocity has a negligible effect, because the limitation is inside the pellet, not in the external film.
