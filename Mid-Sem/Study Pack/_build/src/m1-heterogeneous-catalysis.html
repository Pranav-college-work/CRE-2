Heterogeneous catalysis and catalyst characterisation

Why the rate in a catalytic reactor is not the chemistry alone, what a solid catalyst is made of, and how its surface area, pore volume and pore sizes are measured.

JMS 7-1, 7-2JMS 8-1 to 8-11Class notes pp. 7–36Tutorial 1

1Heterogeneous reactions

A reaction is heterogeneous when more than one phase is present, so reactants must cross a phase boundary before they react. Most industrial reactions are heterogeneous because they need a catalyst, and the catalyst is usually a solid while the reactants are fluids.

The phase boundary brings in physical processes (mass and heat transfer between the bulk fluid and the solid). In a homogeneous reactor these do not exist; in a heterogeneous reactor they can control the rate. That is the single idea behind Chapter 7.

2Global rate of reaction and the seven steps

Design equations (\(V/F_{A0} = \int dX/(-r_A)\) and so on) need a rate written in terms of bulk-fluid concentration and temperature, because those are the only quantities you can measure or specify. A rate that includes the effect of every physical and chemical step, written with bulk conditions, is called the global (or overall, observed) rate. The rate of the chemical steps alone, written with conditions at the surface, is the intrinsic rate.

Three bases for the rate

With a solid present, the same moles-per-second can be divided by reactor volume, catalyst mass or catalyst surface area.

$$-r_V = -\frac{1}{V}\frac{dN_A}{dt}\quad\left[\frac{\text{mol}}{\text{m}^3\,\text{s}}\right]$$
$$-r_W = -\frac{1}{W}\frac{dN_A}{dt}\quad\left[\frac{\text{mol}}{\text{kg cat}\,\text{s}}\right]$$
$$-r_S = -\frac{1}{S}\frac{dN_A}{dt}\quad\left[\frac{\text{mol}}{\text{m}^2\,\text{s}}\right]$$
$$(-r_V)\,V = (-r_W)\,W = (-r_S)\,S \qquad\qquad r_V = \rho_B\, r_W$$

Rates are usually measured per mass of catalyst; multiplying by the bulk density \(\rho_B\) of the packed bed turns them into rates per unit reactor volume for the mole balance.

JMS §7-1: the global rate as resistances in series

Take the irreversible reaction \(\mathrm{A(g)\rightarrow B(g)}\) on non-porous, isothermal catalyst particles, with steps 3–5 lumped into one first-order surface rate. Only two steps involve A, and at steady state their rates are equal (per unit mass of catalyst):

7-1, 7-2$$\underbrace{r_p = k_m a_m\,(C_b - C_s)}_{\text{transport to the surface}} \qquad\qquad \underbrace{r_p = k\, a_m\, C_s}_{\text{reaction at the surface}}$$

Here \(k_m\) is the mass-transfer coefficient (per unit external area), \(a_m\) the external area per unit mass, \(k\) the rate constant per unit surface. \(C_s\) cannot be measured, so eliminate it:

7-3, 7-4$$k_m(C_b - C_s) = kC_s \;\Rightarrow\; C_s = \frac{k_m}{k_m + k}\,C_b \qquad\Rightarrow\qquad r_p = \frac{k\,k_m\,a_m}{k_m + k}\,C_b = \frac{a_m\, C_b}{\dfrac{1}{k} + \dfrac{1}{k_m}}$$
Read it like Ohm's law

Driving force \(C_b\) divided by two resistances in series, \(1/k\) (chemical) and \(1/k_m\) (physical).

  • Reaction control, \(k \ll k_m\): \(C_s \approx C_b\) and \(r_p \approx k a_m C_b\), the intrinsic rate.
  • Diffusion (mass-transfer) control, \(k_m \ll k\): \(C_s \approx 0\) and \(r_p \approx k_m a_m C_b\). The chemistry has dropped out.
  • Intermediate: both terms matter.

Live: why mass transfer hurts more at high temperature

\(k\) follows Arrhenius; \(k_m\) barely changes with temperature. The global constant \(k_g = 1/(1/k + 1/k_m)\) bends from slope \(-E/R\) to almost flat.

How big are these effects? (JMS §7-1 data)
  • \(\mathrm{SO_2}\) oxidation on Pt (465 °C): \(p_{SO_2}\) drops from 0.06 atm in the bulk to 0.040 atm at the surface. Ignoring mass transfer overestimates the rate by 350 %; the surface is also 15 °C hotter than the gas (+31 % rate). Diffusion and heat effects act in opposite directions for this exothermic reaction.
  • \(\mathrm{H_2}\) oxidation on Pt/alumina pellet: bulk 89.9 °C, surface 101 °C, centre 147.7 °C. Using bulk conditions underestimates the rate by 12.5 %; internal heating outweighs internal concentration drop.
  • \(\mathrm{UO_2}\) + HF (non-catalytic): surface 95 °C hotter than the bulk; the combined effect raises the rate by 38 %, while the concentration drop alone lowers it by 19 %.
JMS P7-2 Why does mass-transfer resistance reduce the global rate more at high temperature?

\(k\) grows exponentially with \(T\) but \(k_m\) grows only weakly (roughly as \(T^{0.5\text{–}1}\)). As \(T\) rises, \(1/k\to 0\), so the fixed \(1/k_m\) becomes the dominant resistance. The process drifts into diffusion control and the apparent activation energy falls (see the chart).

JMS P7-3 For an endothermic reaction, do mass- and heat-transfer resistances work together or against each other?

Together. The concentration at the surface is lower than in the bulk (lower rate), and because heat must flow in, the surface is colder than the bulk (lower rate again). For an exothermic reaction the surface is hotter, which partly offsets the concentration drop.

JMS P7-4 Isothermal flow reactor with mass-transfer resistance: does more turbulence raise or lower the global rate? And if exothermic and non-isothermal?

(a) More turbulence raises \(k_m\), lowers \(1/k_m\), so the global rate increases. (b) More turbulence also raises the heat-transfer coefficient, so the surface temperature falls towards the bulk value. The concentration effect raises the rate; the temperature effect lowers it. The net effect depends on which resistance dominates.

3What a catalyst is and what it does (JMS 8-1, 8-2)

A catalyst is a substance that changes the rate of a reaction but emerges chemically unchanged. The term goes back to Berzelius (1836). It works by providing a different reaction path (mechanism) with a lower activation energy.

General characteristics (JMS 8-1) with evidence

CharacteristicEvidence / example
A small amount of catalyst converts a large amount of reactant\(\mathrm{Cu^{2+}}\) at \(10^{-9}\) mol/L appreciably increase the rate of oxidation of sodium sulphide by oxygen (Glasstone)
A catalyst does not change the equilibrium; it speeds forward and reverse reactions equally\(\mathrm{SO_2}\) oxidation over Pt, \(\mathrm{Fe_2O_3}\) and \(\mathrm{V_2O_5}\) all reach the same equilibrium composition
Rate is proportional to active surface area\(\mathrm{H_2 + O_2}\) on Pt: rate proportional to Pt area
Selectivity: a catalyst can steer towards one product among severalEthanol gives ethylene + water over alumina, but acetaldehyde + hydrogen over copper
Class notes: properties of a catalyst
  1. It alters the rate but not the thermodynamics; only the activation energy changes, \(\Delta H\) is the same.
  2. It is not consumed, but it does deactivate.
  3. It provides a high surface area, \(10^2\)–\(10^3\ \mathrm{m^2/g}\).
  4. Supported (active agent such as Pd, Pt, Rh on a support such as \(\mathrm{SiO_2}\), \(\mathrm{Al_2O_3}\), which gives mechanical strength, favourable sites and thermal stability; e.g. \(\mathrm{V_2O_5/SiO_2}\) for \(\mathrm{SO_2\rightarrow SO_3}\)) or unsupported (Fe for \(\mathrm{NH_3}\)).

Turnover frequency and dispersion (class notes; HSF 10.1.3)

$$\text{TOF} = \frac{\text{molecules reacting}}{\text{active site}\cdot\text{s}}\qquad\left(10^{-4}\text{ to }10^{4}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}\right)$$
$$D = \frac{\text{surface metal atoms}}{\text{total metal atoms}}\ [\%]$$

Active site (Taylor): a point on the surface that can form strong chemical bonds with an adsorbed atom or molecule — unsaturated atoms at edges, dislocations, cracks and grain boundaries.

Electronic classification (JMS 8-8, Dowden)

Conductors — metals

Ag, Pt, V, Fe, Ni. Chemisorb by electron transfer; chemisorb \(\mathrm{O_2}\) and \(\mathrm{H_2}\), so good for oxidation–reduction and hydrogenation–dehydrogenation (Pt for \(\mathrm{SO_2}\) oxidation, Ni for hydrogenation). \(\mathrm{O_2}\) and CO are poisons in hydrogenation.

Semiconductors — oxides

NiO, \(\mathrm{Cu_2O}\), ZnO. Electrons move when enough energy is supplied (heating). Same reaction types as metals but at higher temperature; less easily poisoned. \(\mathrm{MoO_3}\), \(\mathrm{Cr_2O_3}\) polymerise olefins.

Insulators — solid acids

Silica gel, alumina, silica–alumina. Activity from carbonium ions at acid sites. Used for cracking, alkylation, isomerisation, polymerisation.

Dual-function catalysts have two active substances close together: Ni on silica–alumina isomerises n-hexane far better than silica–alumina alone (Ni dehydrogenates to hexene, the acid isomerises, Ni re-hydrogenates). The Ziegler–Natta catalyst \(\mathrm{Al(C_2H_5)_3 + TiCl_4}\) makes isotactic polymers; Fe is specific for Fischer–Tropsch.

  • Porous: area from internal pores (silica–alumina: \(0.6\ \mathrm{cm^3/g}\), pore radius 4 nm, \(300\ \mathrm{m^2/g}\)).

ShapeAdvantagesDisadvantagesApplication
SphericalLow manufacturing costRelatively high pressure drop, large diffusion lengthHydrodesulphurisation (HDS), methanation
Irregular granulesLow manufacturing costHigh pressure drop, low surface areaAmmonia, formaldehyde
Pellet (tablet)Regular shape, good strengthLow surface areaCO shift, hydrogenation

Monolith (honeycomb)Very low pressure drop, small diffusion lengthPoor radial heat transferExhaust-gas cleaning

Smaller particles give more external area per volume and shorter diffusion paths, but raise pressure drop. The Ergun equation shows why:

$$\frac{\Delta P}{L} = \frac{150\,\mu\,v\,(1-\varepsilon)^2}{d_p^2\,\varepsilon^3} + \frac{1.75\,\rho\,v^2\,(1-\varepsilon)}{d_p\,\varepsilon^3}$$

Both terms grow as \(d_p\) falls. Higher \(\Delta P\) needs a bigger compressor or pump and costs more, so there is a minimum practical particle size (class notes). Shaped catalysts raise area per volume without shrinking \(d_p\). In a fixed bed the solids stay in place; in a fluidised bed they move.

6Catalyst preparation (JMS 8-9, class notes)

Chemical composition alone does not fix activity: surface area, pore size, particle size and structure all depend on how the catalyst was made. Follow tested recipes exactly.

Precipitation

Add a precipitating agent to aqueous solutions of the components. MgO: precipitate \(\mathrm{MgCO_3}\) from nitrate solution with \(\mathrm{Na_2CO_3}\), wash, dry, calcine. Class example: \(\mathrm{Al(NO_3)_3 + NH_4OH \rightarrow Al(OH)_3\downarrow + NH_4NO_3}\), then calcine to \(\mathrm{Al_2O_3}\).

Gel formation (sol–gel)

A special case of precipitation for colloidal precipitates (silica, alumina). Class notes: (1) a liquid suspension (sol) forms by hydrolysis of a precursor salt; (2) condensation leads to gel formation.

Simple mixing

Mix components with water, mill, dry, calcine; grind and sieve. Mixed MgO–CaO: carbonates wet-milled, extruded, dried, reduced.

Wet vs dry impregnation

Wet: excess solution; transport by diffusion. Dry (incipient wetness): exactly the pore volume of solution; transport by diffusion plus capillary flow.

Dispersion matters more than loading (JMS 8-9)

Ortho-to-para hydrogen conversion over NiO/\(\mathrm{Al_2O_3}\): 5.0 wt % NiO was less active than 0.5 wt %, because the concentrated solution deposited larger Ni particles. The same 5.0 wt % built from ten separate 0.5 wt % depositions was 11 times more active. This is why active area is measured by chemisorption of \(\mathrm{H_2}\) or CO, not inferred from weight per cent.

7Promoters, inhibitors and poisons (JMS 8-10, 8-11)

Exam distinction

Promoter and inhibitor are added deliberately during manufacture. A poison is not added: it comes in with the feed or is produced by the reaction during operation. An accelerator is added to the feed to improve performance (steam in butene dehydrogenation over Fe reduces coke). Accelerators are the counterpart of poisons.

8Physical adsorption vs chemisorption (JMS 8-3, HSF 10.1.3)

Adsorbate: the molecule that attaches. Adsorbent: the surface it attaches to. Surface imperfections raise the surface energy and give the sites (class notes). Adsorption is exothermic, \(\Delta H_{ads} < 0\): the molecule loses freedom, so \(\Delta S < 0\), and \(\Delta G = \Delta H - T\Delta S < 0\) needs \(\Delta H < 0\).

9Adsorption isotherms and the Langmuir treatment (JMS 8-4)

An adsorption isotherm is the amount adsorbed at equilibrium (\(r_{ads} = r_{des}\)) at constant temperature, plotted against pressure (or concentration).

Langmuir's assumptions

  1. The surface is uniform: every site is equally active.
  2. Adsorbed molecules do not interact with each other.
  3. The heat of adsorption is constant, independent of coverage \(\theta\).
  4. Adsorption is limited to a monolayer.
  5. All adsorption occurs by the same mechanism.

Derivation

The rate of adsorption is proportional to the collision rate (\(\propto p\)) and to the fraction of sites still vacant; the rate of desorption is proportional to the fraction covered:

8-1, 8-2$$r_a = k_a\,p\,(1-\theta) \qquad\qquad r_d = k_d\,\theta$$
8-3$$k_a p(1-\theta) = k_d\theta \;\Rightarrow\; \theta = \frac{k_a p}{k_d + k_a p} \;\Rightarrow\; \boxed{\theta = \frac{v}{v_m} = \frac{Kp}{1+Kp}},\qquad K = \frac{k_a}{k_d}$$

\(v\) = volume adsorbed, \(v_m\) = volume of a complete monolayer. Limits: \(Kp \ll 1\) gives \(\theta \approx Kp\) (linear, Henry's law); \(Kp \gg 1\) gives \(\theta\to 1\).

Linear form used by JMS (Eq. 8-8)

$$\frac{p}{v} = \frac{1}{Kv_m} + \frac{p}{v_m}$$

Plot \(p/v\) vs \(p\): slope \(1/v_m\), intercept \(1/(Kv_m)\).

Linear form used in class

$$\frac{1}{v} = \frac{1}{Kv_m}\cdot\frac{1}{p} + \frac{1}{v_m}$$

Plot \(1/v\) vs \(1/p\): slope \(1/(Kv_m)\), intercept \(1/v_m\).

For several gases adsorbing competitively on the same sites (JMS 8-4): \(\theta_A = \dfrac{K_A p_A}{1 + K_A p_A + K_B p_B}\). Chemisorption usually fits Langmuir better than physisorption, because chemisorption stops at a monolayer.

10Surface area by the BET method (JMS 8-5)

Langmuir stops at one layer. Nitrogen near its boiling point builds multilayers, and the Type II knee marks the monolayer. Brunauer, Emmett and Teller extended Langmuir by assuming the first layer has heat of adsorption \(E_1\) and every later layer has the heat of liquefaction \(E_L\). The result:

8-9$$\frac{p}{v\,(p_0 - p)} = \frac{1}{v_m c} + \frac{c-1}{v_m c}\cdot\frac{p}{p_0},\qquad c = \exp\!\left(\frac{E_1 - E_L}{RT}\right)$$

Plot \(y = p/[v(p_0-p)]\) against \(x = p/p_0\), normally in the range \(0.05 < p/p_0 < 0.3\). With intercept \(I\) and slope \(s\):

8-10 to 8-12$$I = \frac{1}{v_m c},\quad s = \frac{c-1}{v_m c}\quad\Rightarrow\quad I + s = \frac{c}{v_m c}\;\Rightarrow\; \boxed{v_m = \frac{1}{I+s}},\qquad c = \frac{s}{I}+1$$

Turn the monolayer volume into an area. \(v_m\) is in \(\mathrm{cm^{3}}\) at STP per gram; \(N_0\) is Avogadro's number; \(\alpha\) is the area covered by one adsorbed molecule:

8-13, 8-14$$S_g = \frac{v_m N_0}{V}\,\alpha,\quad V = 22\,400\ \mathrm{cm^3/mol}\qquad\qquad \alpha = 1.09\left[\frac{M}{N_0\,\rho}\right]^{2/3}$$

\(M\) is the molecular weight and \(\rho\) the density of the adsorbate as liquid at the adsorption temperature. For \(\mathrm{N_2}\) at −195.8 °C (\(\rho = 0.808\ \mathrm{g/cm^3}\)), \(\alpha = 16.2\ \text{Å}^2\) and

8-15$$S_g = 4.35\times10^{4}\ v_m\ \ \mathrm{cm^2/g}\qquad(\text{i.e. } 4.35\,v_m\ \mathrm{m^2/g},\ v_m \text{ in cm}^3\text{(STP)/g})$$
How it is measured (class notes, lecture slide)

Degas the sample to clean the surface. Flow \(\mathrm{N_2}\) (adsorbate) with He (inert carrier) over the sample at liquid-nitrogen temperature and record the uptake at each \(p/p_0\). \(\mathrm{N_2}\) is used because it is cheap and physisorbs on any surface. Catalysts typically have \(10^2\)–\(10^3\) \(\mathrm{m^{2}/g}\) (class notes); a silica–alumina cracking catalyst has about 300 \(\mathrm{m^{2}/g}\) (Fogler).

11Void volume, densities and porosity (JMS 8-6)

Helium–mercury method. Put a weighed sample in a container of known volume.

  • Helium fills the pores and the space around the particles, but cannot enter the solid, so the helium displaced gives the volume of solid.
  • Mercury at atmospheric pressure does not wet the solid and will not fill the pores of most catalysts (at 1.77 psia it enters only voids larger than about 50 \(\mu\mathrm{m}\); see Example 8-6), so the mercury displaced gives the volume of the particles including pores.
$$V_g = \frac{V_{Hg} - V_{He}}{m}\ \ [\mathrm{cm^3/g}]\qquad \rho_s = \frac{m}{V_{He}}\qquad \rho_p = \frac{m}{V_{Hg}}$$

The porosity of the particle is pore volume over total particle volume:

8-16, 8-17$$\varepsilon_p = \frac{m V_g}{m V_g + m/\rho_s} = \frac{V_g\,\rho_s}{V_g\,\rho_s + 1}\qquad\text{and}\qquad \rho_p = \frac{m}{mV_g + m/\rho_s} = \frac{\rho_s}{V_g\rho_s + 1}\ \Rightarrow\ \varepsilon_p = V_g\,\rho_p$$

Also \(\varepsilon_p = 1 - \rho_p/\rho_s\). Typically \(\varepsilon_p \approx 0.5\). With a bed voidage near 0.4, a packed bed is roughly 30 % pore, 30 % solid, 40 % inter-particle space.

Density (class notes, slide 9)Denominator

Bidisperse pellets (Tutorial 1 Q6 = JMS Example 8-5)

Pellets made by compressing porous particles have micropores (inside the particles) and macropores (between particles). With pellet density \(\rho_P\), macropore volume \((V_g)_M\) and micropore volume \((V_g)_\mu\) per gram:

$$\varepsilon_M = (V_g)_M\,\rho_P,\qquad \varepsilon_\mu = (V_g)_\mu\,\rho_P,\qquad \varepsilon_s = 1-\varepsilon_M-\varepsilon_\mu,\qquad \rho_s = \frac{\rho_P}{\varepsilon_s},\qquad \varepsilon_p(\text{particle}) = (V_g)_\mu\,\rho_p$$

The worked numbers are on the Tutorials page.

12Pore-volume distribution (JMS 8-7)

How effective the inner surface is depends not only on how much void there is but on the size of the openings. Real void spaces are irregular (see the porous silver micrographs, JMS Fig. 8-5), so every model represents them as cylindrical pores of radius \(a\). Two methods measure the distribution.

Mercury penetration (porosimetry)

Force balance (Ritter and Drake): applied force on the pore cross-section = surface-tension force around the perimeter.

8-18$$\pi a^2 p = -2\pi a\,\sigma\cos\theta\;\Rightarrow\; a = \frac{-2\sigma\cos\theta}{p}$$

With \(\theta = 140°\) and \(\sigma\) for mercury:

8-19$$a\ (\text{Å}) = \frac{8.75\times10^{5}}{p\ (\text{psi})}$$

About 100 psi fills pores of \(a = 10\,000\) Å; 10 000 psi is needed for \(a = 100\) Å. Easy equipment reaches 100–200 Å; smaller needs high-pressure apparatus.

Bottleneck pores

A large void reached only through a narrow neck fills at the high pressure of the neck and is wrongly counted as small pores. Both methods need every pore to connect to at least one larger pore.

Nitrogen desorption (capillary condensation)

Continue the \(\mathrm{N_2}\) adsorption experiment towards \(p/p_0 \to 1\): multilayers bridge the pores and the pores fill by capillary condensation, smallest pores first. Then lower the pressure step by step. Pores empty when the vapour pressure over their curved meniscus exceeds the imposed pressure. The Kelvin equation, corrected for the adsorbed layer of thickness \(\delta\):

8-20, 8-21$$a - \delta = \frac{-2\sigma V_l\cos\theta}{R_g T\,\ln(p/p_0)},\qquad \delta = A\left(\ln\frac{p_0}{p}\right)^{-1/n}\ \ \text{(Halsey)}$$

\(V_l\) = molar volume of liquid nitrogen, \(\theta = 0°\) because nitrogen wets its own adsorbed film. For \(\mathrm{N_2}\) at −195.8 °C, with \(a\) and \(\delta\) in Å:

8-22, 8-23$$a - \delta = 9.52\left(\log_{10}\frac{p_0}{p}\right)^{-1}\qquad\qquad \delta = 7.34\left(\ln\frac{p_0}{p}\right)^{-1/3}$$

Pelleting pressure squeezes out macropores but hardly touches micropores or area; the micropore peak stays near 20 Å.

Wheeler's mean pore radius

Replace the real particle by \(n\) straight, non-intersecting cylindrical pores, all of radius \(\bar a\) and length \(L\), with the same total surface and pore volume as the real particle of mass \(m_p\):

8-24 to 8-26$$m_p S_g = (2\pi\bar a L)\,n,\qquad m_p V_g = (\pi\bar a^2 L)\,n\qquad\Rightarrow\qquad \boxed{\bar a = \frac{2V_g}{S_g}}$$

Works: monodisperse

Vycor, \(V_g = 0.208\ \mathrm{cm^3/g}\), \(S_g = 90\ \mathrm{m^2/g}\): \(\bar a = 2(0.208)/(90\times10^4) = 46\times10^{-8}\) cm = 46 Å. The volume-average from the full distribution is 45 Å.

Fails: bidisperse

Low-pressure alumina pellet, total \(V_g = 1.64\), \(S_g = 389\): \(\bar a = 84\) Å, where there are almost no pores. Using the micropore volume alone (0.56) gives 29 Å, a sensible micropore average.

13Self-test

Distinguish intrinsic rate and global rate. Which one goes into a reactor design equation, and why?

Intrinsic rate: the rate of the chemical steps (adsorption, surface reaction, desorption) written with conditions at the catalyst surface. Global rate: includes all physical and chemical steps and is written with bulk-fluid conditions. The design equation uses the global rate, because only bulk concentrations and temperatures are measured or specified, and with a global rate the heterogeneous design equations look exactly like homogeneous ones.

A catalyst gives 30 % conversion at equilibrium without a catalyst. What is the equilibrium conversion with it?

Still 30 %. The catalyst lowers the activation energy of forward and reverse steps equally, so \(K = k_f/k_r\) is unchanged; only the time to reach equilibrium changes.

Why does physisorption measure total surface area while chemisorption measures active area?

Physisorption uses non-specific van der Waals forces, so it covers every surface (and forms multilayers whose monolayer point BET can find). Chemisorption forms chemical bonds only with active sites, so its uptake counts active sites.

BET plot: intercept \(0.2\times10^{-3}\), slope \(16.5\times10^{-3}\) \(\mathrm{g/cm^{3}}\). Find \(v_m\), \(c\), \(S_g\) for nitrogen.
$$v_m = \frac{1}{(0.2+16.5)\times10^{-3}} = 59.9\ \mathrm{cm^3/g},\qquad c = \frac{16.5}{0.2}+1 = 83.5,\qquad S_g = 4.35\times59.9 = 261\ \mathrm{m^2/g}$$
50 g sample; helium displaces 18.0 \(\mathrm{cm^{3}}\), mercury 40.0 \(\mathrm{cm^{3}}\). Find \(V_g\), \(\rho_s\), \(\rho_p\), \(\varepsilon_p\).
$$V_g = \frac{40.0-18.0}{50} = 0.44\ \mathrm{cm^3/g},\quad \rho_s = \frac{50}{18.0} = 2.78,\quad \rho_p = \frac{50}{40.0} = 1.25\ \mathrm{g/cm^3},\quad \varepsilon_p = V_g\rho_p = 0.55$$

Check: \(V_g\rho_s/(V_g\rho_s+1) = 1.222/2.222 = 0.55\).

What pore radius does mercury enter at 2000 psi? Why can't nitrogen desorption be used for this pore?

\(a = 8.75\times10^5/2000 = 437.5\) Å. Nitrogen desorption loses sensitivity above about 200 Å because the vapour pressure over such a gently curved meniscus is almost the flat-surface value.

Why is \(\bar a = 2V_g/S_g\) meaningless for an alumina pellet?

The pellet is bidisperse: a narrow micropore peak (about 20 Å) and a broad macropore peak (about \(10^{3}\)–\(10^{4}\) Å). A single average falls between the peaks, where there are almost no pores. Apply the formula to each pore range separately, or integrate the distribution.

Give one example each of a promoter, an inhibitor, a poison and an accelerator.

Promoter: \(\mathrm{Al_2O_3}\) in Fe ammonia catalyst (prevents sintering). Inhibitor: halogen compound in Ag/\(\mathrm{Al_2O_3}\) for ethylene oxide (suppresses complete oxidation). Poison: sulphur compounds on Ni hydrogenation catalysts, or coke on cracking catalysts. Accelerator: steam in the butene feed to Fe dehydrogenation catalyst.

Why does the reaction rate per kg rise when particles are made smaller, and what stops you making them very small?

Smaller particles have more external area per mass and shorter diffusion paths, so less film and pore resistance. But the Ergun pressure drop grows as \(1/d_p^2\) (laminar) and \(1/d_p\) (turbulent), raising compression cost; very small particles are also weak and can be carried out of a fixed bed.

Which IUPAC isotherm would you expect for (a) activated carbon with micropores only, (b) a mesoporous silica?

(a) Type I: the micropores fill at low \(p/p_0\) and there is no room for multilayers, so uptake plateaus. (b) Type IV: multilayer build-up then capillary condensation in the mesopores, with a hysteresis loop between adsorption and desorption branches.
