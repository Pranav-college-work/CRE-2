Heterogeneous catalysis and catalyst characterisation

Why the rate in a catalytic reactor is not the chemistry alone, what a solid catalyst is made of, and how its surface area, pore volume and pore sizes are measured.

JMS 7-1, 7-2JMS 8-1 to 8-11Class notes pp. 7–36Tutorial 1

1Heterogeneous reactions

A reaction is heterogeneous when more than one phase is present, so reactants must cross a phase boundary before they react. Most industrial reactions are heterogeneous because they need a catalyst, and the catalyst is usually a solid while the reactants are fluids.

The phase boundary brings in physical processes (mass and heat transfer between the bulk fluid and the solid). In a homogeneous reactor these do not exist; in a heterogeneous reactor they can control the rate. That is the single idea behind Chapter 7.

2Global rate of reaction and the seven steps

Design equations (V/FA0=∫dX/(−rA)V/F_{A0} = \int dX/(-r_A)V/FA0​=∫dX/(−rA​) and so on) need a rate written in terms of bulk-fluid concentration and temperature, because those are the only quantities you can measure or specify. A rate that includes the effect of every physical and chemical step, written with bulk conditions, is called the global (or overall, observed) rate. The rate of the chemical steps alone, written with conditions at the surface, is the intrinsic rate.

How big are these effects? (JMS §7-1 data)
  • SO2\mathrm{SO_2}SO2​ oxidation on Pt (465 °C): pSO2p_{SO_2}pSO2​​ drops from 0.06 atm in the bulk to 0.040 atm at the surface. JMS P7-2 Why does mass-transfer resistance reduce the global rate more at high temperature?

kkk grows exponentially with TTT but kmk_mkm​ grows only weakly (roughly as T0.5–1T^{0.5\text{–}1}T0.5–1). As TTT rises, 1/k→01/k\to 01/k→0, so the fixed 1/km1/k_m1/km​ becomes the dominant resistance. The process drifts into diffusion control and the apparent activation energy falls (see the chart).

JMS P7-3 For an endothermic reaction, do mass- and heat-transfer resistances work together or against each other?

Together. The concentration at the surface is lower than in the bulk (lower rate), and because heat must flow in, the surface is colder than the bulk (lower rate again). For an exothermic reaction the surface is hotter, which partly offsets the concentration drop.

JMS P7-4 Isothermal flow reactor with mass-transfer resistance: does more turbulence raise or lower the global rate? And if exothermic and non-isothermal?

(a) More turbulence raises kmk_mkm​, lowers 1/km1/k_m1/km​, so the global rate increases. (b) More turbulence also raises the heat-transfer coefficient, so the surface temperature falls towards the bulk value. The concentration effect raises the rate; the temperature effect lowers it. The net effect depends on which resistance dominates.

3What a catalyst is and what it does (JMS 8-1, 8-2)

A catalyst is a substance that changes the rate of a reaction but emerges chemically unchanged. The term goes back to Berzelius (1836). It works by providing a different reaction path (mechanism) with a lower activation energy.

Electronic classification (JMS 8-8, Dowden)

Conductors — metals

Ag, Pt, V, Fe, Ni. Chemisorb by electron transfer; chemisorb O2\mathrm{O_2}O2​ and H2\mathrm{H_2}H2​, so good for oxidation–reduction and hydrogenation–dehydrogenation (Pt for SO2\mathrm{SO_2}SO2​ oxidation, Ni for hydrogenation). O2\mathrm{O_2}O2​ and CO are poisons in hydrogenation.

Semiconductors — oxides

NiO, Cu2O\mathrm{Cu_2O}Cu2​O, ZnO. Electrons move when enough energy is supplied (heating). Same reaction types as metals but at higher temperature; less easily poisoned. MoO3\mathrm{MoO_3}MoO3​, Cr2O3\mathrm{Cr_2O_3}Cr2​O3​ polymerise olefins.

Insulators — solid acids

Silica gel, alumina, silica–alumina. Activity from carbonium ions at acid sites. Used for cracking, alkylation, isomerisation, polymerisation.

Dual-function catalysts have two active substances close together: Ni on silica–alumina isomerises n-hexane far better than silica–alumina alone (Ni dehydrogenates to hexene, the acid isomerises, Ni re-hydrogenates). The Ziegler–Natta catalyst Al(C2H5)3+TiCl4\mathrm{Al(C_2H_5)_3 + TiCl_4}Al(C2​H5​)3​+TiCl4​ makes isotactic polymers; Fe is specific for Fischer–Tropsch.

6Catalyst preparation (JMS 8-9, class notes)

Chemical composition alone does not fix activity: surface area, pore size, particle size and structure all depend on how the catalyst was made. Follow tested recipes exactly.

Precipitation

Add a precipitating agent to aqueous solutions of the components. MgO: precipitate MgCO3\mathrm{MgCO_3}MgCO3​ from nitrate solution with Na2CO3\mathrm{Na_2CO_3}Na2​CO3​, wash, dry, calcine. Class example: Al(NO3)3+NH4OH→Al(OH)3↓+NH4NO3\mathrm{Al(NO_3)_3 + NH_4OH \rightarrow Al(OH)_3\downarrow + NH_4NO_3}Al(NO3​)3​+NH4​OH→Al(OH)3​↓+NH4​NO3​, then calcine to Al2O3\mathrm{Al_2O_3}Al2​O3​.

Gel formation (sol–gel)

A special case of precipitation for colloidal precipitates (silica, alumina). Class notes: (1) a liquid suspension (sol) forms by hydrolysis of a precursor salt; (2) condensation leads to gel formation.

Simple mixing

Mix components with water, mill, dry, calcine; grind and sieve. Mixed MgO–CaO: carbonates wet-milled, extruded, dried, reduced.

Wet vs dry impregnation

Wet: excess solution; transport by diffusion. Dry (incipient wetness): exactly the pore volume of solution; transport by diffusion plus capillary flow.

Dispersion matters more than loading (JMS 8-9)

Ortho-to-para hydrogen conversion over NiO/Al2O3\mathrm{Al_2O_3}Al2​O3​: 5.0 wt % NiO was less active than 0.5 wt %, because the concentrated solution deposited larger Ni particles. The same 5.0 wt % built from ten separate 0.5 wt % depositions was 11 times more active. This is why active area is measured by chemisorption of H2\mathrm{H_2}H2​ or CO, not inferred from weight per cent.

7Promoters, inhibitors and poisons (JMS 8-10, 8-11)

Exam distinction

Promoter and inhibitor are added deliberately during manufacture. A poison is not added: it comes in with the feed or is produced by the reaction during operation. An accelerator is added to the feed to improve performance (steam in butene dehydrogenation over Fe reduces coke). Accelerators are the counterpart of poisons.

9Adsorption isotherms and the Langmuir treatment (JMS 8-4)

An adsorption isotherm is the amount adsorbed at equilibrium (rads=rdesr_{ads} = r_{des}rads​=rdes​) at constant temperature, plotted against pressure (or concentration).

Langmuir's assumptions

  1. The surface is uniform: every site is equally active.
  2. Adsorbed molecules do not interact with each other.
  3. The heat of adsorption is constant, independent of coverage θ\thetaθ.
  4. Adsorption is limited to a monolayer.
  5. All adsorption occurs by the same mechanism.

Derivation

The rate of adsorption is proportional to the collision rate (∝p\propto p∝p) and to the fraction of sites still vacant; the rate of desorption is proportional to the fraction covered:

11Void volume, densities and porosity (JMS 8-6)

Helium–mercury method. Put a weighed sample in a container of known volume.

  • Helium fills the pores and the space around the particles, but cannot enter the solid, so the helium displaced gives the volume of solid.
  • Mercury at atmospheric pressure does not wet the solid and will not fill the pores of most catalysts (at 1.77 psia it enters only voids larger than about 50 μm\mu\mathrm{m}μm; see Example 8-6), so the mercury displaced gives the volume of the particles including pores.

13Self-test

Distinguish intrinsic rate and global rate. Which one goes into a reactor design equation, and why?

Intrinsic rate: the rate of the chemical steps (adsorption, surface reaction, desorption) written with conditions at the catalyst surface. Global rate: includes all physical and chemical steps and is written with bulk-fluid conditions. The design equation uses the global rate, because only bulk concentrations and temperatures are measured or specified, and with a global rate the heterogeneous design equations look exactly like homogeneous ones.

A catalyst gives 30 % conversion at equilibrium without a catalyst. What is the equilibrium conversion with it?

Still 30 %. The catalyst lowers the activation energy of forward and reverse steps equally, so K=kf/krK = k_f/k_rK=kf​/kr​ is unchanged; only the time to reach equilibrium changes.

Why does physisorption measure total surface area while chemisorption measures active area?

Physisorption uses non-specific van der Waals forces, so it covers every surface (and forms multilayers whose monolayer point BET can find). Chemisorption forms chemical bonds only with active sites, so its uptake counts active sites.

Give one example each of a promoter, an inhibitor, a poison and an accelerator.

Promoter: Al2O3\mathrm{Al_2O_3}Al2​O3​ in Fe ammonia catalyst (prevents sintering). Inhibitor: halogen compound in Ag/Al2O3\mathrm{Al_2O_3}Al2​O3​ for ethylene oxide (suppresses complete oxidation). Poison: sulphur compounds on Ni hydrogenation catalysts, or coke on cracking catalysts. Accelerator: steam in the butene feed to Fe dehydrogenation catalyst.

Why does the reaction rate per kg rise when particles are made smaller, and what stops you making them very small?

Smaller particles have more external area per mass and shorter diffusion paths, so less film and pore resistance. But the Ergun pressure drop grows as 1/dp21/d_p^21/dp2​ (laminar) and 1/dp1/d_p1/dp​ (turbulent), raising compression cost; very small particles are also weak and can be carried out of a fixed bed.

Which IUPAC isotherm would you expect for (a) activated carbon with micropores only, (b) a mesoporous silica?

(a) Type I: the micropores fill at low p/p0p/p_0p/p0​ and there is no room for multilayers, so uptake plateaus. (b) Type IV: multilayer build-up then capillary condensation in the mesopores, with a hysteresis loop between adsorption and desorption branches.
