Q1

Define the rate of reaction.

1 mark

The rate of reaction is an intensive measure of how fast a chemical change proceeds: the number of moles of a species formed or consumed per unit time, per unit of a chosen reference quantity (volume of reacting fluid, mass of catalyst, interfacial area, and so on).

Based on unit volume of reacting fluid — the definition used throughout homogeneous kinetics:

−rA = −1V dNAdt = (moles of A disappearing) (volume)⁢(time)

units: mol L−1 s−1

For a constant-density (constant-volume) system this reduces to the familiar form, since CA = NA/V:

−rA= −dCAdt

Points that matter

  • It is intensive, not extensive. The rate is a property of the reacting mixture at a given composition and temperature — it does not depend on the type or size of reactor. The reactor only decides where on the composition scale the rate is evaluated.
  • Sign convention. −rA is written for a reactant so that it comes out positive; rR is positive for a product.
  • Rates of different species are linked by stoichiometry. For aA + bB → rR + sS:
    −rAa= −rBb= rRr= rSs
  • Other bases are used when the reaction is not homogeneous — per unit mass of catalyst, −r′A = −(1/W) dNA/dt; per unit interfacial area, −r″A; per unit volume of reactor, −r ⋮A. They are related by the amount of each phase present.
Q2

What is Arrhenius’ Law? Explain with the required expression.

2 marks

For most reactions the rate can be split into a temperature-dependent part and a composition-dependent part:

−rA= k(T) ⁢f(Ci)

Arrhenius’ Law states that the temperature dependence of the rate constant k is exponential:

Arrhenius equation
k=k0 ⁢ e−EaRT

Meaning of each term

  • k0 — the pre-exponential or frequency factor. It carries the units of k (which depend on the reaction order) and represents the rate constant extrapolated to infinite temperature: the collision frequency together with the orientation (steric) requirement.
  • Ea — the activation energy, J mol−1. The minimum energy a colliding pair must carry for reaction to occur.
  • R — the gas constant, 8.314 J mol−1 K−1.
  • T — absolute temperature, K.

The exponential factor e−Ea/RT is, from the Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution, the fraction of molecular collisions energetic enough to react. Raising T raises that fraction sharply, which is why rates are so temperature-sensitive.

Working forms

(i) Linearised form — used to extract Ea from data. A plot of ln k against 1/T is a straight line of slope −Ea/R and intercept ln k0:

ln⁡k= ln⁡k0 − EaR ⁢1T

(ii) Two-temperature form — for finding Ea from two rate constants, or k at a new temperature:

ln⁡ k2k1 = EaR ( 1T1 − 1T2 )

Consequence: a reaction with a high activation energy is strongly temperature-sensitive, while one with a low Ea is comparatively insensitive. This is the basis for choosing operating temperature to favour a desired reaction in a multiple-reaction system.

Q3

If −rA = −(dCA/dt) = 0.2 mol/(lit·s) when CA = 1 mol/lit, what is the rate when CA = 10 mol/lit if the order of the reaction is not known?

1 mark

Assume an nth-order rate law at fixed temperature:

−rA= k⁢CAn

The single data point fixes k, because CA = 1 makes CAn = 1 for every n:

0.2=k⁢(1)n ⇒ k=0.2

units of k: (mol/lit)1−n s−1

At CA = 10 mol/lit:

−rA= 0.2×(10)n
Answer

The rate cannot be determined. One rate–concentration pair fixes only the constant k; it says nothing about how the rate scales with concentration. Since n is unknown, −rA = 0.2 × 10n is indeterminate — it spans several orders of magnitude over the physically common range of n.

Q4

What is the difference between space-time and residence time?

2 marks

Space-time τ is a design parameter. It is the time required to process one reactor volume of feed, measured at specified inlet conditions:

τ= Vv0 = CA0VFA0 , space velocity=1τ

v0 = volumetric feed rate evaluated at a chosen reference state (usually reactor inlet)

Residence time t is a physical quantity: the actual time a given element of fluid spends inside the reactor. In a real vessel different elements take different paths, so residence time is a distribution, E(t), whose mean is

t¯= ∫0∞ t⁢E(t)⁢dt and in general t¯= ∫0V dVv(V)
Side-by-side comparison
 Space-time τResidence time t
NatureDefined / calculated design quantityMeasured physical quantity of a fluid element
Based onVolumetric flow at inlet (reference) conditions, v0Actual local flow rate inside the reactor, v(V)
Single value?Yes — one number per reactorNo — a distribution E(t); only its mean is a single number
Depends on reaction?No (V and v0 only)Yes, if the reaction changes density or moles
Formulaτ = V / v0t̄ = ∫ dV / v

When are they equal?

  • Constant-density systems (all liquids; gases with εA = 0 at constant T and P): v = v0 everywhere, so τ = t̄. This is the usual case in this course.
  • They differ when density varies — gas-phase reactions with a change in mole number (εA ≠ 0), or with a temperature or pressure gradient along the reactor. Then v ≠ v0 and t̄ ≠ V/v0. For an expanding gas (εA > 0) the fluid speeds up, so t̄ < τ.
  • Even when the mean residence time equals τ, the spread can be enormous. In an ideal PFR every element stays exactly τ; in an ideal CSTR the exit-age distribution is E(t) = (1/τ)e−t/τ, so some molecules leave almost immediately and others linger — despite both reactors having the same τ.

This last point is exactly why two reactors with identical space-time can give very different conversions, and it is the subject of RTD analysis.

Q5

Aqueous A reacts to form R (A → R). In the first minute in a batch reactor its concentration drops from CA0 = 2.03 mol/liter to CAf = 1.97 mol/liter. Find the rate equation if the kinetics are second order in A.

2 marks
Given — batch reactor; aqueous (so constant density, V = const); CA0 = 2.03 mol/L, CAf = 1.97 mol/L at t = 1 min; order n = 2.
Find — the complete rate equation.

Step 1 — Set up and integrate

For a constant-volume batch reactor with second-order kinetics:

−rA= −dCAdt =k⁢CA2

Separating variables and integrating from (CA0, 0) to (CA, t):

− ∫CA0CA dCACA2 = k∫0tdt ⇒ 1CA − 1CA0 =kt

Step 2 — Substitute the data

k= 1CAf−1CA0 t = CA0−CAf CA0⁢CAf⁢t
k= 2.03−1.97 (2.03)(1.97)(1) = 0.063.9991 =0.0150

equivalently 1/1.97 − 1/2.03 = 0.507614 − 0.492611 = 0.015003 L mol−1, over t = 1 min

Answer — rate equation
−rA= 0.015⁢CA2 [ mol L−1  min−1 ]

with k = 0.0150 L mol−1 min−1  (= 2.50 × 10−4 L mol−1 s−1)

Sanity check. The conversion here is tiny (only 3 %), so the rate is nearly constant over the minute. The crude average-rate estimate gives −rA ≈ ΔCA/Δt = 0.06 mol/(L·min) at a mean concentration of 2.00 mol/L, so k ≈ 0.06/2.002 = 0.0150 — identical to three figures, confirming the integration.

Q6

1 liter/min of liquid containing A and B (CA0 = 0.10, CB0 = 0.01 mol/liter) flows into a mixed reactor of volume V = 1 liter. The stoichiometry is unknown. The outlet contains A, B and C (CAf = 0.02, CBf = 0.03, CCf = 0.04 mol/liter). Find the rate of reaction of A, B and C.

2 marks
Given — mixed-flow (CSTR), steady state, liquid (constant density); v0 = 1 L/min, V = 1 L.
Key insight — you do not need the stoichiometry. A CSTR mole balance gives each rate directly.

Step 1 — Space-time

τ=Vv0 =1 L1 L/min =1 min

Step 2 — Steady-state mole balance on a CSTR

For any species i: in − out + generation = 0. Because the vessel is perfectly mixed, the composition everywhere inside equals the outlet composition, so the rate is uniform and comes straight out of the balance — no integration:

v0Ci0 − v0Cif +riV=0 ⇒ −ri= Ci0−Cif τ

Step 3 — Apply to each species

Rates evaluated at the reactor (= outlet) composition, τ = 1 min
Species Ci0 Cif (Ci0 − Cif)/τ Result
A0.100.02+0.08 −rA = 0.08 — A is consumed
B0.010.03−0.02 rB = +0.02 — B is formed (net)
C00.04−0.04 rC = +0.04 — C is formed
Answer — all rates in mol L−1 min−1
  • A: −rA = 0.08  (disappearing at 0.08 mol L−1 min−1)
  • B: −rB = −0.02, i.e. rB = +0.02  (B is produced on balance, even though it is fed — its outlet concentration is higher than its inlet)
  • C: −rC = −0.04, i.e. rC = +0.04  (produced; CC0 = 0 since C is absent from the feed)

Comments

  • Why this works without stoichiometry. A CSTR operates entirely at its exit composition, so the mole balance is algebraic. The rate is obtained by simple difference — this is precisely why the mixed-flow reactor is the preferred device for collecting kinetic data on complicated systems.
  • Deducing an overall stoichiometry. The net rates are in the ratio  rA : rB : rC = −0.08 : +0.02 : +0.04 = −4 : 1 : 2, which is consistent with an overall change 4A → B + 2C. Note this is only the net (observed) stoichiometry; the underlying mechanism may well involve B being consumed and regenerated in several steps.
  • The sign convention does all the bookkeeping: a positive (Ci0 − Cif) means consumption, a negative one means net production.
Q7

A real reactor modelled as two different reactor systems, with τPFR = τCSTR = 1 min, k = 1.0 m³/(kmol·min) and CA0 = 1.0 kmol/m³. Find the conversion for (a) CSTR followed by PFR and (b) PFR followed by CSTR, for reaction order n = 1 and n = 2. Comment on the result.

5 marks
Given — liquid-phase (constant density), isothermal, τ = 1 min for each unit, so the total space-time is 2 min in both arrangements — that is what makes the comparison fair. CA0 = 1.0 kmol/m³.
Note on units — the constant k = 1.0 m³/(kmol·min) is the second-order value. Units of k must change with order, so for n = 1 we take k = 1.0 min−1 (numerically 1.0 in both cases, as intended by the problem).

Design equations used

Constant density, so X = 1 − CA/CA0 and each unit is handled on a concentration basis, taking the outlet of one unit as the inlet of the next.

CSTR: τ= Cin−Cout k⁢Coutn PFR: τ= ∫CoutCin dCk⁢Cn

Case n = 1  (k = 1.0 min−1)

The integrated forms are Cout = Cin/(1 + kτ) for the CSTR and Cout = Cine−kτ for the PFR. With kτ = 1:

First order — stage-by-stage concentrations, kmol/m³

(a)CSTR1/2 = 0.5000PFR0.5 e−1 = 0.18390.8161
(b)PFRe−1 = 0.3679CSTR0.3679/2 = 0.18390.8161
Result for n = 1

X = 0.816 (81.6 %) for both arrangements — the order makes no difference. The reason is structural: for first order each unit applies a multiplicative factor to the concentration, and multiplication commutes:

Cout= CA0 ⁢ (11+kτm) ⁢ e−kτp

… and this expression contains no information about which unit came first.

Case n = 2  (k = 1.0 m³ kmol−1 min−1)

Now the CSTR equation becomes a quadratic, kτCout2 + Cout − Cin = 0, and the PFR integrates to 1/Cout − 1/Cin = kτ.

System (a) — CSTR then PFR. CSTR: 1 = (1 − C1)/C12 ⇒ C12 + C1 − 1 = 0 ⇒ C1 = (√5 − 1)/2 = 0.6180. PFR: 1/C2 = 1/0.6180 + 1 = 2.6180 ⇒ C2 = 0.3820.

System (b) — PFR then CSTR. PFR: 1/C1 = 1 + 1 = 2 ⇒ C1 = 0.5000. CSTR: 1 = (0.5 − C2)/C22 ⇒ C22 + C2 − 0.5 = 0 ⇒ C2 = (√3 − 1)/2 = 0.3660.

Comment on the result

  1. For n = 1 the sequence is irrelevant (both give 81.61 %). A first-order rate depends linearly on concentration, so no arrangement can exploit a concentration advantage.
  2. For n = 2 the sequence matters: PFR-first wins, 63.40 % against 61.80 % — a gain of 1.60 percentage points, or about 2.6 % relative, for exactly the same total reactor volume. Free conversion, purely from piping order.
  3. The general rule (Levenspiel): for n > 1 put the plug-flow unit first and the mixed-flow unit last; for n < 1 reverse it; for n = 1 it does not matter. The physical reason is that a CSTR instantly dilutes its feed down to exit concentration and reacts entirely at that lowest concentration. When n > 1 the rate is very sensitive to concentration, so you want to keep CA high for as long as possible — which means not squandering the rich, fast-reacting feed on a back-mixed vessel. Send it through the PFR first, and give the CSTR the already-dilute stream, where the penalty for back-mixing is small.
  4. Bounds check. With the same total τ = 2 min and n = 2, a single PFR would give X = 0.667 (the best any arrangement can do) and a single CSTR X = 0.500 (the worst). Both mixed arrangements fall between: 0.500 < 0.618 < 0.634 < 0.667. ✓

Why, graphically

On a Levenspiel plot of 1/(−rA) against X, space-time is area. A PFR consumes the area under the curve; a CSTR consumes a rectangle whose height is set by its outlet conditions. The shaded areas below are equal (1 min each, 2 min total) in both panels — but panel (b) reaches further to the right.

Quantitatively, the area a CSTR wastes above the curve is 0.382 min in system (a) but only 0.268 min in system (b). That difference of 0.114 min of “usefully spent” space-time is exactly what buys the extra 1.6 percentage points of conversion.
