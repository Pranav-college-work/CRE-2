Q1 — Dissociative chemisorption of N₂ and H₂ on Fe

Both gases dissociate on adsorption, so each molecule needs a pair of adjacent vacant sites S:

N2 + 2S ⇌ 2 N·S      H2 + 2S ⇌ 2 H·S

Step 1 — equilibrium for each gas. At equilibrium the rates of adsorption and desorption are equal, so for nitrogen

KN₂ = CN·S2 pN₂ Cv2   ⟹   CN·S = Cv √(KN₂ pN₂)

The square root is the fingerprint of dissociation — the surface concentration goes as √p, not p. Identically for hydrogen:

CH·S = Cv √(KH₂ pH₂)

Step 2 — site balance. Every dissociatively adsorbed molecule takes two sites out of circulation, so the total site concentration Ct is

Ct = Cv + 2 CN·S + 2 CH·S

Step 3 — eliminate Cv. Substituting the two equilibrium expressions,

Ct = Cv[ 1 + 2√(KN₂pN₂) + 2√(KH₂pH₂) ]   ⟹   Cv = Ct 1 + 2√(KN₂pN₂) + 2√(KH₂pH₂)

and putting this back into CN·S = Cv√(KN₂pN₂):

CN·S = Ct √(KN₂ pN₂) 1 + 2√(KN₂pN₂) + 2√(KH₂pH₂)   ∎
Note on the factor 2. The 2's come from Step 2, where each adsorbed species is counted as blocking a pair of sites. If instead one books the site balance per adsorbed atom (one N atom on one site), it reads Ct = Cv + CN·S + CH·S and the 2's drop out. Both conventions appear in the literature; the form asked for here is the dual-site one. Either way the key physics is unchanged: √p dependence, and NH₃ synthesis is inhibited by H₂ because adsorbed H competes for the sites N needs.

Q2 — Langmuir isotherm for CO₂ at 25 °C

θ = qqmax = Kp1 + Kp   ⟹   q = qmax K p1 + Kp

Method. Two parameters fitted by minimising SSE = Σ(qcalc − qexp)² directly on the non-linear equation (Excel → Data → Solver, GRG Nonlinear, Min, changing qmax and K). The starting guess is taken from the linearised form p/q = 1/(qmaxK) + p/qmax, whose slope gives 1/qmax. The linear fit is only a starting guess — it distorts the error weighting.

Adsorbentqmax [mmol/g]K [mmHg⁻¹] R²RMSEAARD %
Activated carbon2.6300.01450 0.94820.11866.97
Silica gel2.9710.001368 0.99780.02316.97

Interpretation.

  • Silica gel — R² = 0.9978, residuals random. Langmuir works. But Kp ≪ 1 over the whole range, so the data never approach saturation and qmax is a long extrapolation.
  • Activated carbon — R² = 0.948 only, and the residuals are systematic rather than random. Langmuir assumes one uniform site type; activated carbon has a wide distribution of pore sizes and binding energies, so the real isotherm rises faster at low p and flattens more slowly than a single-site Langmuir can. A heterogeneous isotherm (Tóth / Freundlich / Sips) is needed — which is exactly what Q3 uses.
  • Carbon binds CO₂ ≈ 11× more strongly than silica (K = 0.0145 vs 0.00137 mmHg⁻¹): at 100 mmHg the carbon is already ≈ 59 % covered, the silica only ≈ 12 %.

Q3 — Tóth and multi-site Langmuir on zeolite 13X

Tóth:  qqmax = K p [ 1 + (K p)n ]1/n       Multi-site Langmuir:  qqmax = K p ( 1 − q/qmax )a

Both collapse to Langmuir at n = 1 / a = 1. Tóth's n measures surface heterogeneity (n < 1); the multi-site a is the number of sites one molecule occupies.

Fitting strategy (this is the part that matters)

  • All three temperatures are fitted together. qmax, n and a belong to the adsorbent–adsorbate pair and are temperature-independent; all of the temperature dependence sits in K. So one regression per gas: 5 parameters (qmax, n, K298, K308, K323) against all the data. Fitting each isotherm separately would let qmax drift with T and make the van 't Hoff plot meaningless.
  • The multi-site model is implicit in q — θ = Kp(1−θ)a cannot be rearranged. But θexp = qexp/qmax is known, so pcalc = θ/[K(1−θ)a] is explicit. It is therefore regressed in the pressure direction, minimising Σ(ln pcalc − ln pexp)² — plain Excel formulas, no iteration. The p = 0 point is dropped (ln 0).
  • qmax and a are strongly correlated in the multi-site model — turned loose, Solver walks a past 20 with an equally unphysical qmax. qmax is therefore fixed at the Tóth value: the same gas on the same zeolite must saturate at the same loading. Only a and the three K are regressed.

ΔHads from the van 't Hoff plot

K = K0 exp( −ΔHads / RT )   ⟹   ln K = ln K0 − ΔHadsR ·1T

A straight line of ln K against 1/T has slope = −ΔHads/R, so ΔHads = −R × slope (Excel: =SLOPE(lnK, 1/T)).

ΔHads (CO₂) ≈ −65 to −68 kJ/mol  ·   ΔHads (CH₄) ≈ −16 kJ/mol

What the numbers say

  • Tóth n. n(CO₂) = 0.24 is far below 1 — CO₂ sees a very heterogeneous surface, because it binds first to the strong, few extra-framework Na⁺ cations and only then to the rest of the cage. n(CH₄) = 0.61 is much closer to 1: non-polar CH₄ has no preferred cation site, so the surface looks nearly uniform to it.
  • Multi-site a. a(CO₂) = 6.1 vs a(CH₄) = 2.2 — the linear, quadrupolar CO₂ effectively ties up several sites, CH₄ behaves as a small compact adsorbate. (Since qmax was pinned to make a identifiable, read it as a shape parameter, not a literal site count.)
  • Both models agree on ΔHads to within a few percent — that agreement, not the R² of either fit alone, is the real evidence the number is physical and not an artefact of one equation.
  • Why 13X separates CO₂ from CH₄. |ΔHads| for CO₂ is ≈ 4.2× that of CH₄: the CO₂ quadrupole couples strongly to the electric field of the Na⁺ cations, CH₄ only through weak dispersion. Hence 13X for biogas upgrading and natural-gas sweetening in PSA/TSA cycles. The large |ΔH| for CO₂ also means the bed heats up noticeably on adsorption — which is why a real adsorber is modelled non-isothermally.
  • Both ΔH are negative: adsorption is exothermic, so loading falls as T rises — exactly what the three isotherms show.
